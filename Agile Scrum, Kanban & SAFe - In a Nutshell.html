    📖 Agile Introduction

    intro overview

    Definition

    Agile is an iterative way of managing projects and developing software that makes it easier for teams to deliver value to their customers more quickly and effectively.

    An agile team is to deliver small but consumable increments of work rather than wagering everything on a "big bang" launch.

    Agile: Incremental + Iterative
    Agile emphasizes delivering work in small, iterative cycles that allow for continuous feedback and adaptation.

    Agile Methodology

    Agile methodology is a project management approach that prioritizes cross-functional collaboration and continuous improvement. It divides projects into smaller phases and guides teams through cycles of planning, execution, and evaluation.

    Key Benefits of Agile

    ⚡ Faster Time to Market

    Deliver value incrementally and get products to market faster.

    🎯 Higher Quality

    Continuous testing and feedback improve product quality.

    🤝 Better Collaboration

    Cross-functional teams work together more effectively.

    📈 Increased Flexibility

    Adapt quickly to changing requirements and market conditions.

    😊 Greater Satisfaction

    Both customers and team members experience higher satisfaction.

    🔍 Transparency

    Stakeholders have visibility into progress and challenges.

    Agile Methodologies

    Some of the Agile methodologies are as follows:

    • Extreme Programming (XP) - Focuses on engineering practices like pair programming, test-driven development, and continuous integration
    • Kanban - Visualizes workflow, limits work in progress, and focuses on continuous delivery
    • Lean - Emphasizes eliminating waste, amplifying learning, and delivering fast
    • Scrum - Scrum is the most popular Agile framework because of its simplicity, flexibility, teamwork focus, empirical process control, and widespread adoption.
    • Crystal - A family of methodologies that adapts to team size and project criticality
    • Feature-Driven Development (FDD) - Builds software feature by feature with short iterations
    • Dynamic Systems Development Method (DSDM) - Provides a comprehensive framework for Agile project management

    When to Use Agile

    Agile is particularly effective when:

    • Requirements are expected to change or evolve
    • Customer feedback is critical to success
    • Time to market is a competitive advantage
    • Innovation and experimentation are valued
    • Cross-functional collaboration is possible
    • The team can work in close proximity (physical or virtual)

    The Agile Manifesto is supported by 12 principles that guide Agile practices:

    Our highest priority is to satisfy the customer through early and continuous delivery of valuable software.

    2. Welcome Change

    Welcome changing requirements, even late in development. Agile processes harness change for the customer's competitive advantage.

    3. Frequent Delivery

    Deliver working software frequently, from a couple of weeks to a couple of months, with a preference to the shorter timescale.

    4. Collaboration

    Business people and developers must work together daily throughout the project.

    5. Motivated Individuals

    🎯 Scrum

    scrum definition
    Scrum (n): A framework within which people can address complex adaptive problems, while productively and creatively delivering products of the highest possible value.

    Origin and History

    Scrum was formally introduced in 1995 by Ken Schwaber and Jeff Sutherland who co-created the framework. The term "Scrum" comes from rugby, where a scrum is a formation used to restart play, symbolizing teamwork and unified effort toward a common goal.

    📚 The Scrum Guide

    The official definition and rules of Scrum are maintained in "The Scrum Guide" by Schwaber and Sutherland, which is updated periodically to reflect lessons learned and best practices.

    Key Characteristics

    Scrum is:

    • Lightweight - Easy to understand and implement with minimal overhead
    • Simple to understand - Clear roles, events, and artifacts that are easy to learn
    • Difficult to master - Requires discipline, commitment, and cultural change to execute effectively

    Core Concepts

    📆 Sprint

    A time-box of one month or less during which a "Done", usable, and potentially releasable product increment is created.

    🗃 Product Backlog

    An ordered list of everything that is known to be needed in the product, owned by the Product Owner.

    🗂 Sprint Backlog

    The set of Product Backlog items selected for the Sprint, plus a plan for delivering the product increment.

    📦 Increment

    The sum of all Product Backlog items completed during a Sprint and all previous Sprints.

    Why Scrum Works

    • Empirical Process Control - Based on transparency, inspection, and adaptation
    • Self-Organization - Teams choose how best to accomplish their work
    • Collaboration - Emphasizes team interaction and shared responsibility
    • Value-Based Prioritization - Focus on delivering the highest value features first
    • Time-Boxing - Fixed-length iterations create rhythm and predictability
    • Iterative Development - Build incrementally and incorporate feedback continuously

    🧪 Scrum Theory

    theory empiricism

    Foundation: Empiricism

    Scrum is founded on empirical process control theory, or empiricism.

    Empiricism asserts that knowledge comes from experience and making decisions based on what is known.

    Empiricism has three key aspects:

    • Observation - Teams observe the actual results of their work
    • Experience - Learning comes from doing and reflecting on outcomes
    • Experimentation - Try new approaches and learn from results

    Defined vs. Empirical Process Control

    ❌ Defined Process Control

    • Plan everything upfront
    • Execute according to plan
    • Works for predictable, repeatable processes
    • Examples: Manufacturing, Construction
    • Problem: Software development is complex and unpredictable

    🧪 Empirical Process Control

    • Make a hypothesis (plan)
    • Execute and observe results
    • Inspect outcomes and adapt
    • Works for complex, unpredictable work
    • Advantage: Embraces change and uncertainty

    Approach

    Scrum employs an iterative, incremental approach to optimize predictability and control risk.

    🔄 The Inspect and Adapt Cycle

    Scrum builds in frequent opportunities to inspect progress and adapt the plan:

    • Daily Scrum - Inspect daily progress toward Sprint Goal
    • Sprint Review - Inspect the product increment
    • Sprint Retrospective - Inspect the process and team dynamics
    • Sprint Planning - Adapt the plan based on what was learned

    This approach allows teams to:

    • Learn from experience and observations
    • Adapt to changing requirements
    • Make data-driven decisions
    • Continuously improve processes
    • Minimize risks through frequent inspection
    • Validate assumptions early and often
    • Respond quickly to feedback
    • Build the right product incrementally

    Complexity and Scrum

    Scrum acknowledges that software development is complex, not complicated:

    • Complicated - Many parts, but predictable (e.g., a car engine)
    • Complex - Unpredictable interactions, emergent behavior (e.g., software systems, human teams)

    For complex work, empiricism is the only way to make reliable predictions and effective decisions.

    🏛️ Scrum Pillars

    pillars foundation

    Scrum is built on three fundamental pillars that support empirical process control. These pillars are interdependent and reinforce each other:

    🔍 Transparency

    Giving visibility to the significant aspects of the process to those responsible for the outcome.

    Everyone must have a shared understanding of the work and standards being used.

    🔎 Inspection

    Timely checks on the progress toward a sprint goal to detect undesirable variances.

    Regular inspection prevents waste and identifies problems early.

    🔧 Adaptation

    Adjusting a process as soon as possible to minimize any further deviation or issues.

    Teams must be empowered to make adjustments when needed.

    Deep Dive into Each Pillar

    🔍 Transparency - Creating a Shared Reality

    Transparency means that all significant aspects of the process must be visible to those responsible for the outcome. This requires:

    • Common Language - All participants share the same understanding of terms
    • Definition of Done - Shared understanding of what "complete" means
    • Visible Artifacts - Product Backlog, Sprint Backlog, and Increment are accessible to everyone
    • Open Communication - No hidden agendas or information hoarding

    ⚠️ Without Transparency:

    • Decisions are based on flawed or incomplete information
    • Problems remain hidden until they become critical
    • Trust erodes within the team and with stakeholders
    • Inspection and adaptation become ineffective

    ✅ Practical Examples:

    • Daily stand-ups where everyone shares progress and impediments
    • Sprint boards visible to the entire team and stakeholders
    • Clear acceptance criteria for every user story
    • Burndown charts showing real-time progress

    🔎 Inspection - Frequent Progress Checks

    Inspection requires that Scrum artifacts and progress toward agreed goals are inspected frequently and diligently to detect variances. Key points:

    • Frequent - Not so frequent as to impede work, but often enough to detect problems
    • Skilled Inspectors - Those performing the inspection must have the knowledge and skills
    • At the Point of Work - Inspection should happen where the work is being done
    • Non-Intrusive - Inspection should not get in the way of the work itself

    📊 Scrum's Inspection Opportunities:

    • Daily Scrum - Daily inspection of progress toward Sprint Goal
    • Sprint Review - Inspection of the product Increment
    • Sprint Retrospective - Inspection of the team's process and practices
    • Backlog Refinement - Ongoing inspection and refinement of backlog items

    🎯 What to Inspect:

    • Are we building the right product?
    • Are we meeting our Sprint Goal?
    • Are there any impediments blocking progress?
    • Is the quality of our work acceptable?
    • Are we working effectively as a team?

    🔧 Adaptation - Responding to Change

    Adaptation means that if an inspector determines that aspects of a process deviate outside acceptable limits and the resulting product is unacceptable, adjustments must be made quickly. Key aspects:

    • Timely - Adaptations should be made as soon as possible to minimize deviation
    • Empowered Teams - Teams must have the authority to make changes
    • Evidence-Based - Adaptations should be based on inspection findings
    • Continuous - Adaptation is an ongoing process, not a one-time event

    🔄 Types of Adaptation:

    • Product Adaptation - Adjusting what is being built based on feedback
    • Process Adaptation - Improving how the team works together
    • Technical Adaptation - Refining technical practices and tools
    • Planning Adaptation - Adjusting plans based on new information

    ⚙️ Adaptation in Action:

    • Adjusting the Sprint Backlog during Daily Scrum
    • Revising the Product Backlog based on Sprint Review feedback
    • Implementing process improvements from Sprint Retrospective
    • Re-estimating work based on actual velocity

    The Pillar Relationship

    🔗 Interdependence: The three pillars work together. Without transparency, inspection is flawed. Without inspection, adaptation is unfocused. Without adaptation, inspection is wasted.

    💎 Scrum Values

    values principles

    Scrum is supported by five core values that guide the team's work, actions, and behavior. When these values are embodied by the Scrum Team, the empirical pillars of transparency, inspection, and adaptation come to life:

    🚀 COURAGE

    Scrum Team members have courage to do the right thing and work on tough problems.

    🎯 FOCUS

    Everyone focuses on the work of the Sprint and the goals of the Scrum Team.

    🏛️ COMMITMENT

    People personally commit to achieving the goals of the Scrum Team.

    🤝 RESPECT

    Scrum Team members respect each other to be capable, independent people.

    🔓 OPENNESS

    The Scrum Team and its stakeholders agree to be open about all the work and the challenges with performing the work.

    Deep Dive into Each Value

    🚀 Courage - Doing the Right Thing

    Courage enables team members to:

    • Tackle challenging and complex problems
    • Admit mistakes and learn from failures
    • Speak up when something isn't right
    • Challenge the status quo and suggest improvements
    • Deliver honest feedback, even when it's difficult
    • Push back on unrealistic expectations

    ✅ Courage in Practice:

    • A developer admits they don't know how to solve a problem and asks for help
    • The team refuses to commit to a Sprint Goal they know is unrealistic
    • Someone raises a concern about product direction in Sprint Review
    • The team decides to refactor code even though it slows visible progress

    🎯 Focus - Concentrating on What Matters

    Focus means:

    • Concentrating on the Sprint Goal and Sprint Backlog
    • Minimizing work in progress (WIP)
    • Avoiding distractions and context switching
    • Prioritizing the most valuable work
    • Saying "no" to work outside the current Sprint
    • Protecting the team from interruptions

    ✅ Focus in Practice:

    • The team declines a request to work on urgent bugs during a Sprint
    • Daily Scrums stay focused on the Sprint Goal
    • The team limits the number of items they pull into the Sprint
    • Dedicated time blocks for deep work without meetings

    🏛️ Commitment - Personal Accountability

    Commitment involves:

    • Committing to the Sprint Goal, not just individual tasks
    • Doing whatever it takes to help the team succeed
    • Following through on commitments
    • Supporting teammates when they need help
    • Being present and engaged in Scrum events
    • Continuously improving skills and practices

    📌 Important Note:

    Commitment is to the Sprint Goal and team success, NOT to completing every item in the Sprint Backlog. The team commits to doing their best, not to guaranteeing specific outcomes.

    ✅ Commitment in Practice:

    • A developer helps test someone else's work to meet the Sprint Goal
    • The team stays late to resolve a critical impediment together
    • Everyone attends Sprint ceremonies prepared and engaged
    • Team members take ownership of problems, not just their tasks

    🤝 Respect - Valuing Each Other

    Respect means:

    • Treating everyone as capable, independent professionals
    • Valuing different perspectives and expertise
    • Listening actively without interrupting
    • Assuming positive intent
    • Respecting decisions made by others in their role
    • Being considerate of others' time and commitments

    ✅ Respect in Practice:

    • Product Owner respects the team's technical decisions
    • Developers respect the Product Owner's prioritization
    • Team members credit others for their contributions
    • Disagreements are handled professionally and constructively
    • Everyone's voice is heard in team discussions

    🔓 Openness - Transparency in Action

    Openness includes:

    • Being honest about progress, challenges, and mistakes
    • Sharing information freely with the team and stakeholders
    • Being receptive to feedback and new ideas
    • Discussing problems openly rather than hiding them
    • Admitting when you don't know something
    • Making work visible to everyone

    ✅ Openness in Practice:

    • Team openly discusses impediments in Daily Scrum
    • Sprint Review includes honest assessment of what wasn't completed
    • Retrospectives create safe space for candid discussion
    • Product Backlog is visible and accessible to all stakeholders
    • Team shares both successes and failures with stakeholders

    Living the Values

    🌟 Key Insight: These values are not just nice-to-have ideals. They are essential for Scrum to work effectively. When team members embody these values, they create an environment where empirical process control thrives, leading to better products and happier teams.

    Values in Action - Daily Behaviors

    • In Daily Scrum: Show up on time (Respect), be honest about progress (Openness), help teammates (Commitment), stay focused on Sprint Goal (Focus), raise impediments (Courage)
    • In Sprint Planning: Challenge unrealistic expectations (Courage), commit to achievable goals (Commitment), respect estimates (Respect), share concerns openly (Openness), stay focused on value (Focus)
    • In Sprint Review: Demonstrate with confidence (Courage), listen to feedback (Respect), be honest about results (Openness), focus on value delivered (Focus), commit to improvements (Commitment)
    • In Sprint Retrospective: Share constructively (Openness), listen without defensiveness (Respect), identify improvements (Focus), implement changes (Commitment), address difficult topics (Courage)

    📚 The Six Key Processes

    The Scrum process consists of six interconnected activities that work together to create a potentially shippable product increment each Sprint:

    📋 1. Product Backlog Creation & Management

    The Product Backlog is an ordered list of everything that is known to be needed in the product. It is the single source of requirements for any changes to be made to the product.

    📝 Contains:

    • User Stories - Features described from user perspective ("As a [user], I want [goal], so that [benefit]")
    • Business Value - Prioritized by value to customers and business impact
    • Acceptance Criteria - Clear definition of done for each item
    • Estimates - Relative sizing using story points or ideal days
    • Technical Debt - Items to improve code quality and architecture
    • Bugs - Defects that need fixing prioritized by severity
    • Spikes - Research or investigation work to reduce uncertainty

    👤 Owner:

    Product Owner is responsible for content, prioritization, and accessibility

    🔄 Evolution:

    Dynamic and constantly evolving based on feedback and changing needs

    🔄 2. Backlog Refinement (Grooming)

    The backlog is continuously refined and prioritized based on new insights, feedback, and changing business needs. This is an ongoing activity throughout the Sprint, typically consuming no more than 10% of Development Team capacity.

    🎯 Activities include:

    • Breaking down large items into smaller, manageable pieces that fit within a Sprint
    • Adding detail - estimates, acceptance criteria, and technical notes
    • Re-ordering items based on priority, dependencies, and risk
    • Removing items that are no longer relevant or valuable
    • Clarifying requirements with stakeholders and subject matter experts
    • Identifying dependencies and technical constraints

    👥 Participants:

    Product Owner, Development Team, sometimes Scrum Master

    ⏱️ Frequency:

    Ongoing activity, often mid-Sprint refinement sessions

    📅 3. Sprint Planning

    Sprint Planning initiates the Sprint by laying out the work to be performed. The entire Scrum Team collaborates to plan the Sprint and create a shared understanding of the Sprint Goal.

    ⏱️ Time-box:

    Maximum 8 hours for a one-month Sprint (proportionally less for shorter Sprints)

    👥 Participants:

    Entire Scrum Team (Product Owner, Scrum Master, Development Team)

    ❓ The team answers three key questions:

    • Why is this Sprint valuable? → Sprint Goal
    • What can be Done this Sprint? → Sprint Backlog selection
    • How will the chosen work get done? → Task breakdown & estimation

    📤 Output:

    Sprint Goal + Sprint Backlog + Definition of Done + Plan for delivering the Increment

    🏃 4. Sprint Execution (The Heart of Scrum)

    The Sprint is a fixed time-box (typically 2-4 weeks) during which a "Done", usable, and potentially releasable product Increment is created. Sprints are the heartbeat of Scrum where ideas are turned into value.

    🎯 Sprint Characteristics:

    • Fixed Duration - Same length every time for predictability and rhythm
    • Sprint Goal - Clear objective that provides guidance and flexibility
    • No Harmful Changes - That endanger the Sprint Goal without team agreement
    • Quality Goals - Do not decrease; Definition of Done remains constant
    • Scope Clarification - Can be clarified and re-negotiated with Product Owner as more is learned
    • Cancellation - Only Product Owner can cancel if Sprint Goal becomes obsolete (rare)

    🗣️ Daily Scrum (Daily Stand-up):

    • Time-box: 15 minutes maximum, same time and place every day
    • Purpose: Inspect progress toward Sprint Goal and adapt Sprint Backlog as necessary
    • Format: Development Team synchronizes activities and creates plan for next 24 hours
    • Three Key Questions:
      • What did I do yesterday that helped meet the Sprint Goal?
      • What will I do today to help meet the Sprint Goal?
      • Do I see any impediment that prevents me or the team from meeting the Sprint Goal?
    • Not for problem solving - Issues identified are addressed immediately after with interested parties
    • Builds team commitment - Improves communication, identifies impediments, promotes quick decision-making

    👁️ 5. Sprint Review (Inspect the Product)

    The Sprint Review is held at the end of the Sprint to inspect the Increment and adapt the Product Backlog if needed. This is a working session, not just a presentation or demo. It's an informal meeting to foster collaboration.

    ⏱️ Time-box:

    Maximum 4 hours for a one-month Sprint (proportionally less for shorter)

    👥 Attendees:

    Scrum Team + Key Stakeholders invited by Product Owner

    📋 Agenda includes:

    • What's Done: Product Owner explains what Product Backlog items are "Done" and not done
    • Demonstration: Development Team demonstrates the work and answers questions about the Increment
    • Discuss Backlog: Product Owner discusses current state of Product Backlog and projections
    • Collaborate: Entire group collaborates on what to do next based on the review
    • Review Context: Review of timeline, budget, potential capabilities, and marketplace changes
    • Next Steps: Discussion of next probable Product Backlog items and release dates

    📤 Output:

    Revised Product Backlog + Probable items for next Sprint + Feedback incorporated

    🔍 6. Sprint Retrospective (Inspect the Process)

    The Sprint Retrospective is an opportunity for the Scrum Team to inspect itself and create a plan for improvements to be enacted during the next Sprint. It occurs after Sprint Review and before next Sprint Planning.

    ⏱️ Time-box:

    Maximum 3 hours for a one-month Sprint (typically 45 min - 1.5 hours)

    👥 Attendees:

    Scrum Team only (safe, trusted environment for honest feedback)

    🎯 Purpose:

    Inspect how the last Sprint went regarding:

    • People: Team dynamics, collaboration, communication
    • Relationships: How team members interact and support each other
    • Process: Scrum practices, workflows, ceremonies
    • Tools: Technology, software, equipment used

    ❓ Key Questions:

    • What went well in the Sprint? (Continue doing)
    • What could be improved? (Start doing or stop doing)
    • What will we commit to improve in the next Sprint? (Action items)

    📤 Output:

    Identified improvements + At least one high-priority process improvement committed for next Sprint

    💡 Key Insight

    These six processes work together in a continuous loop. Each Sprint is a mini-project with a goal, planning, execution, review, and improvement phase. The cycle repeats, with each iteration building on lessons learned from previous Sprints, creating a continuous improvement culture and delivering value incrementally.

    Sprint Timeline Example (2-Week Sprint)

    • Day 1 (Monday): Sprint Planning (4 hours) → Sprint begins
    • Days 2-9: Daily Scrum every morning (15 min) + Development work
    • Day 10 (Friday Week 2):
      • Morning: Sprint Review (2 hours)
      • Afternoon: Sprint Retrospective (1.5 hours)
      • Next Sprint begins immediately (or Monday)

    Key Concepts

    ⛳ Sprint Goal

    An objective that provides guidance to the team on why they are building the Increment. It provides flexibility regarding functionality implemented within the Sprint.

    ✅ Definition of Done

    A shared understanding of what it means for work to be complete. Ensures transparency and quality. All work must meet this definition before it's considered done.

    📦 Increment

    The sum of all Product Backlog items completed during a Sprint and all previous Sprints. The Increment must be in useable condition and meet the Definition of Done.

    🔥 Velocity

    The amount of work a team completes in a Sprint, measured in story points. Used for forecasting and Sprint planning. Becomes more accurate over time.

    🔁 Continuous Cycle: Scrum is not a one-time process. Each Sprint builds on the previous one, with continuous learning, adaptation, and improvement. The cycle repeats until the product is complete or the project ends.

    📆 Scrum Events (Ceremonies)

    events ceremonies
    Scrum prescribes four formal events for inspection and adaptation. These events are specifically designed to enable the transparency and inspection required. Failure to operate any events as prescribed results in lost opportunities to inspect and adapt.

    The Four Core Scrum Events

    📋 Sprint Planning

    When: Beginning of Sprint

    Duration:
    2-week: 4 hrs  ·  4-week: 8 hrs

    Plan the work to be performed in the Sprint. Creates the Sprint Goal and Sprint Backlog.

    🗣️ Daily Standup

    When: Every day

    Duration: 15 minutes

    Inspect progress toward Sprint Goal and adapt Sprint Backlog as necessary.

    ✅ Sprint Review

    When: End of Sprint

    Duration:
    2-week: 2 hrs  ·  4-week: 4 hrs

    Inspect the Increment and adapt the Product Backlog. Demo to stakeholders.

    🔄 Sprint Retrospective

    When: After Sprint Review

    Duration:
    2-week: 1.5 hrs  ·  4-week: 3 hrs

    Inspect how the last Sprint went regarding people, relationships, process, and tools.

    Event Characteristics

    • Time-boxed - Each event has a maximum duration to ensure efficiency
    • Formal opportunities - Prescribed occasions for inspection and adaptation
    • Required attendance - Specific team members must participate in each event
    • Regular cadence - Events occur at predictable intervals
    • Focused purpose - Each event has specific goals and outcomes

    The Sprint as a Container

    The Sprint itself is considered an event that acts as a container for all other events. Each Sprint is a fixed time-box of one month or less, during which a "Done", useable, and potentially releasable product Increment is created.

    Sprints have consistent durations throughout a development effort. A new Sprint starts immediately after the conclusion of the previous Sprint.

    Benefits of Formal Events

    • 📊 Transparency - Everyone knows when key activities occur
    • 🔍 Inspection - Regular opportunities to examine work and progress
    • 🔧 Adaptation - Timely adjustments based on inspection results
    • ⏱️ Predictability - Minimizes need for ad-hoc meetings
    • 🎯 Focus - Each event has a clear purpose and outcome
    • 🤝 Collaboration - Brings team together at right times

    ⚠️ Important

    Scrum Events are used to create regularity and to minimize the need for meetings not defined in Scrum. All events are time-boxed, meaning they have a maximum duration. Once a Sprint begins, its duration is fixed and cannot be shortened or lengthened.

    📆 Sprint Planning

    ceremony planning
    Sprint Planning initiates the Sprint by laying out the work to be performed for the Sprint. This resulting plan is created by the collaborative work of the entire Scrum Team.

    ⏱️ Time Box

    • 2 weeks sprint: 4 hours maximum
    • 4 weeks sprint: 8 hours maximum

    👥 Attendees

    • Scrum Master (facilitates the meeting)
    • Product Owner (clarifies the details of the product backlog items)
    • Development Team (defines the work and effort necessary to meet the sprint commitment)

    📅 When

    Before starting with a new sprint - Sprint Planning happens at the beginning of every Sprint.

    🎯 Sprint Planning Addresses Three Key Questions

    ❓ WHAT can be delivered?

    The Development Team works to forecast the functionality that will be developed during the Sprint. The Product Owner discusses the objective that the Sprint should achieve and the Product Backlog items that would achieve the Sprint Goal.

    🔧 HOW will the work get done?

    Having selected the Product Backlog items for the Sprint, the Development Team discusses how it will build this functionality into a "Done" product Increment. The work may be of varying size or estimated effort.

    📝 Key Inputs

    📚 Product Backlog

    Prioritized list of features and requirements

    📦 Current Product State

    Latest product increment and its capabilities

    📊 Current Product Velocity

    Historical team capacity and performance

    🚧 Constraints

    Technical, business, or resource limitations

    📤 Key Outputs

    ⛳ Sprint Goal

    A short, one- or two-sentence description of what the team plans to achieve during the sprint. Written collaboratively by the team and the product owner.

    📋 Sprint Backlog

    A list of the product backlog items the team commits to delivering plus the list of tasks necessary to delivering those product backlog items.

    Best Practices

    • ✅ Ensure Product Backlog is refined and ready before planning
    • ✅ Product Owner should clearly communicate priorities and goals
    • ✅ Development Team should ask clarifying questions
    • ✅ Break down large items into smaller, manageable tasks
    • ✅ Consider team capacity and velocity from previous sprints
    • ✅ Create a realistic commitment that the team can achieve
    • ✅ Define clear acceptance criteria for each item

    🙋 Daily Standup (Daily Scrum)

    ceremony daily
    A daily stand-up meeting is a short organizational meeting that is held each day. The meeting, generally limited to between five and 15 minutes long, is sometimes referred to as a stand-up, a morning roll-call or a daily Scrum.

    ⏱️ Time Box

    ⏱️

    15 Minutes Maximum

    📍 Key Characteristics

    📍

    Same Place

    Consistent location every day

    ⏰

    Same Time

    Fixed time each day

    🎯

    Focus on 3 Questions

    Structured format

    👥 Participants

    👨‍💼 Facilitated by Scrum Master

    Ensures the meeting stays on track and within time-box

    👥 Full Team Presence

    All Development Team members attend and participate

    🎯 The 3 Main Questions

    1️⃣ What I did yesterday?

    Share what you completed since the last Daily Scrum

    2️⃣ What I'll do today?

    Communicate your plans for work today

    3️⃣ What's in my way?

    Identify any blockers or impediments

    Purpose and Benefits

    • 🎯 Synchronize activities - Ensure everyone knows what's happening
    • 📊 Inspect progress - Check advancement toward Sprint Goal
    • 🔧 Adapt the plan - Adjust Sprint Backlog as needed
    • 🚧 Identify impediments - Surface blockers early
    • 🤝 Promote collaboration - Foster team communication
    • ⚡ Quick decision making - Address issues rapidly

    Best Practices

    • ✅ Stand up (if possible) to keep the meeting short and energetic
    • ✅ Start on time, every time - respect everyone's schedule
    • ✅ Keep it brief - no deep dives or problem-solving
    • ✅ Focus on progress toward Sprint Goal
    • ✅ Take detailed discussions offline after the standup
    • ✅ Update the task board during or right after the meeting
    • ✅ Make impediments visible for Scrum Master to address

    Common Anti-Patterns to Avoid

    • ❌ Treating it as a status report to the Scrum Master or Product Owner
    • ❌ Going over the time-box (15 minutes)
    • ❌ Solving problems during the standup
    • ❌ Only talking when directly asked
    • ❌ Not identifying blockers or asking for help
    • ❌ Discussing topics not related to Sprint Goal

    🎥 Sprint Review

    ceremony review
    The Sprint Review is held at the end of the Sprint to inspect the Increment and adapt the Product Backlog if needed. This is an informal meeting, not a status meeting, and the presentation of the Increment is intended to elicit feedback and foster collaboration.

    ⏱️ Time Box

    • 2 weeks sprint: 1.5 hours (2-4 hours)
    • 4 weeks sprint: 3 hours (2-4 hours)

    👥 Attendees

    • Scrum Master - Facilitates and ensures event happens
    • Product Owner - Explains what has been "Done" and what hasn't
    • Scrum Team - Discusses what went well, problems, and solutions
    • Stakeholders and Sponsors - Invited by Product Owner
    • Customer - May attend to provide feedback

    📅 When

    At the end of sprint - Held before the Sprint Retrospective

    🎯 Key Activities

    ✅ Demo "Done" Functionalities

    Development Team demonstrates the work that it has "Done" and answers questions about the Increment. Focus on working software.

    📋 Review Product Backlog

    Product Owner discusses Product Backlog as it stands. Projects likely completion dates based on progress to date.

    ❓ Discuss Next Sprint Planning

    Entire group collaborates on what to do next. Review timeline, budget, potential capabilities, and marketplace.

    📊 Key Outputs & Objectives

    • 📦 Inspect the Increment - Review completed work from Sprint
    • 🔍 Compare work committed vs. completed - Assess sprint success
    • 🎯 Review key decisions made - Document important choices
    • 📈 Review project metrics - Code coverage, velocity, burndown, etc.
    • 🎬 Demo of the work itself - Show actual working software
    • 🔮 Priority review for next iteration/sprint - Plan ahead
    • 📝 Updated Product Backlog - Refined based on feedback

    Sprint Review Flow

    Increment
    Show completed work
    →
    2-4 hours
    Time-boxed meeting
    →
    "Done" Functionalities
    Working features
    Updated Product Backlog
    Refined priorities
    ←
    Next Sprint Planning Date
    Schedule next steps

    Best Practices

    • ✅ Keep it informal - This is a working session, not a formal presentation
    • ✅ Show working software - Focus on "Done" increments only
    • ✅ Encourage feedback - Create a safe environment for honest input
    • ✅ Invite key stakeholders - Include those who can provide valuable feedback
    • ✅ Update Product Backlog - Capture new insights and requirements
    • ✅ Celebrate success - Acknowledge team accomplishments
    • ✅ Be transparent - Discuss what didn't get done and why

    ❌ This is NOT a Retrospective

    Sprint Review focuses on the product (what was built), while Sprint Retrospective focuses on the process (how it was built). Don't confuse the two!

    👩‍⚕️ Sprint Retrospective

    ceremony retrospective
    The Sprint Retrospective is an opportunity for the Scrum Team to inspect itself and create a plan for improvements to be enacted during the next Sprint. It occurs after the Sprint Review and prior to the next Sprint Planning.

    ⏱️ Time Box

    • 2 weeks sprint: 2 hours (30 min - 3h)
    • 4 weeks sprint: 4 hours (30 min - 3h)

    👥 Attendees

    • Scrum Master - Facilitates the retrospective
    • Product Owner - Participates as a team member
    • Scrum Team - All development team members

    📅 When

    At the end of sprint - After Sprint Review, before next Sprint Planning

    🎯 The Three Core Questions

    ✅ What went well?

    Identify successes and positive practices to continue

    ⚠️ What could be improved?

    Surface problems and areas needing attention

    🔧 How can we improve it?

    Create actionable improvement plans

    🔍 Focus Areas

    🔍 Self-Analysis on How to Work

    Examine team dynamics, communication patterns, and collaboration effectiveness. Discuss how the team works together.

    ⚠️ Problem Analysis and Improved Aspects

    Identify issues that arose during the Sprint and discuss potential solutions. Analyze what could be done better.

    🔧 Framework Improvements

    Discuss improvements to tools, processes, and practices. Consider changes to Definition of Done, development practices, etc.

    Alternative Question Format

    Instead of asking what went well and what didn't go well, the following questions may be asked:

    • 🟢 What should we start doing? - New practices to adopt
    • 🔴 What should we stop doing? - Practices to discontinue
    • 🟡 What should we continue to do? - Effective practices to maintain

    Key Outcomes

    • 📝 Identified improvements - Concrete actions for next Sprint
    • 🎯 Action items with owners - Clear responsibility for changes
    • 📊 Metrics review - Examine velocity, quality, team health
    • 🤝 Improved team relationships - Better communication and trust
    • 🛠️ Process adjustments - Refined ways of working
    • 📚 Lessons learned - Captured knowledge for future reference

    The Retrospective Prime Directive

    🧘 The Retrospective Prime Directive

    "Regardless of what we discover, we understand and truly believe that everyone did the best job they could, given what they knew at the time, their skills and abilities, the resources available, and the situation at hand."

    — Norm Kerth

    Best Practices

    • ✅ Create a safe environment - Team members should feel comfortable being honest
    • ✅ Focus on process, not people - Discuss systems and practices, not individuals
    • ✅ Be specific - Vague feedback leads to vague improvements
    • ✅ Limit improvement items - Pick 1-3 actionable improvements for next Sprint
    • ✅ Follow up - Review previous retrospective actions at the start
    • ✅ Rotate facilitation - Different perspectives keep it fresh
    • ✅ Time-box discussions - Keep the meeting focused and productive
    • ✅ Document outcomes - Capture action items and decisions

    Common Retrospective Techniques

    • 🌟 Start, Stop, Continue - Identify practices to start, stop, and continue
    • ⛵ Sailboat - Wind (helps), anchors (hinders), rocks (risks), island (goal)
    • 😊😐😞 Mad, Sad, Glad - Categorize feelings about the Sprint
    • 📈 4 L's - Liked, Learned, Lacked, Longed For
    • 🌹🥀💡 Rose, Thorn, Bud - Positives, negatives, opportunities
    • ⚓ Speed boat - What's slowing us down?

    ⚠️ Avoid These Anti-Patterns

    • ❌ Blaming individuals for problems
    • ❌ Skipping retrospective when things go well
    • ❌ No follow-through on action items
    • ❌ Management dictating improvements
    • ❌ Rehashing old issues without solutions
    • ❌ Making it a complaints session

    👩‍💻 The Product Owner (PO)

    role product owner
    The Product Owner is responsible for maximizing the value of the product resulting from work of the Development Team and is the sole person responsible for managing the Product Backlog.

    Core Responsibility

    The Product Owner is accountable for effective Product Backlog management, which includes:

    • Developing and explicitly communicating the Product Goal
    • Creating and clearly communicating Product Backlog items
    • Ordering Product Backlog items
    • Ensuring that the Product Backlog is transparent, visible, and understood

    📝 Key Responsibilities

    📝 Product Backlog Management

    • Clearly expressing Product Backlog items
    • Ordering items to best achieve goals and missions
    • Optimizing the value of the work the Development Team performs
    • Ensuring visibility, transparency, and clarity
    • Ensuring the team understands items to the level needed

    🎯 Value Maximization

    • Understanding product planning in empirical environment
    • Helping the Scrum Team understand clear Product Backlog items
    • Understanding and practicing agility
    • Facilitating Scrum events as requested or needed

    👥 Stakeholder Management

    • Ensuring goals, scope, and product domain are understood
    • Helping the Product Owner arrange the backlog to maximize value
    • Collaborating with stakeholders to gather requirements
    • Communicating vision and strategy

    Authority and Accountability

    ⚠️ Important: One Person, One Voice

    The Product Owner is one person, not a committee. The Product Owner may represent the needs of many stakeholders in the Product Backlog, but those wanting to change a Product Backlog item's priority must address the Product Owner.

    For the Product Owner to succeed, the entire organization must respect their decisions. The Product Owner's decisions are visible in the content and ordering of the Product Backlog. No one can force the Development Team to work from a different set of requirements.

    Skills and Qualities

    A successful Product Owner should have:

    • Domain Knowledge - Deep understanding of the business and users
    • Availability - Accessible to the team and stakeholders
    • Decision-Making - Ability to make timely, informed decisions
    • Vision - Clear understanding of product direction and goals
    • Communication - Excellent stakeholder management skills
    • Empowerment - Authority to make product decisions
    • Collaboration - Works closely with Development Team

    Common Anti-Patterns to Avoid

    • ❌ Proxy Product Owner - Having someone other than the PO make decisions
    • ❌ Absent Product Owner - Not being available to the team
    • ❌ Committee-based decisions - Multiple people acting as PO
    • ❌ Technical Product Owner - Focusing on how instead of what and why
    • ❌ Order taker - Simply writing down whatever stakeholders request

    👷‍♀️ Developers (formerly Development Team)

    role developers
    The Developers (called "Development Team" in pre-2020 Scrum Guide) are the people in the Scrum Team who are committed to creating any aspect of a usable Increment each Sprint. They are professionals who do the work of delivering a potentially releasable Increment of "Done" product at the end of each Sprint.
    📘 Scrum Guide 2020 Update: The term "Development Team" was updated to "Developers" to emphasize that everyone on the Scrum Team develops the product, and to remove any implied hierarchy. The team is now called "Developers" and they are accountable as a whole for creating a usable Increment.

    🎯 Key Characteristics

    🤝 Self-Managing

    No one tells the Developers how to turn Product Backlog into Increments of potentially releasable functionality. The team internally decides who does what, when, and how (evolved from "self-organizing" in 2020).

    🛠️ Cross-Functional

    Developers have all the skills necessary to create a product Increment. No dependencies on people outside the team.

    🚫 No Sub-Teams

    Scrum recognizes no titles and no sub-teams for Developers, regardless of work domains that need to be addressed. There are no hierarchies within the Developers.

    👥 Collective Accountability

    Accountability belongs to the Developers as a whole. Individual members may have specialized skills, but as a unit, the Developers are accountable for creating a usable Increment that meets the Definition of Done.

    💼 Developers' Responsibilities

    • Create a Plan (Sprint Backlog) - Design Sprint Backlog during Sprint Planning
    • Instill Quality - Ensure adherence to the Definition of Done
    • Adapt the Plan Daily - Adjust Sprint Backlog toward Sprint Goal each day
    • Self-Manage - Decide who does what, when, and how
    • Deliver Usable Increment - Create "Done" increments every Sprint
    • Hold Each Other Accountable - Collectively accountable as professionals
    • Continuous Improvement - Identify and implement improvements
    • Technical Excellence - Apply engineering best practices

    Team Size

    📈 Optimal Team Size

    10 or fewer people is the optimal Scrum Team size (including Product Owner and Scrum Master).

    • Small enough to remain nimble and large enough to complete significant work within a Sprint
    • Too large? If Scrum Teams become too large, they should consider reorganizing into multiple cohesive Scrum Teams, each focused on the same product
    • Typically 3-9 Developers plus Product Owner and Scrum Master

    Note: The 2020 Scrum Guide changed from "3-9 Development Team members" to "10 or fewer people in the Scrum Team" for greater flexibility.

    Skills and Qualities

    Effective Development Team members demonstrate:

    • Technical Excellence - Strong skills in their domain
    • Collaboration - Work well together toward common goals
    • T-Shaped Skills - Deep expertise in one area, broad knowledge in others
    • Commitment - Dedicated to Sprint Goals and team success
    • Courage - Willing to tackle tough problems and admit mistakes
    • Transparency - Open about progress, challenges, and impediments
    • Adaptability - Comfortable with change and uncertainty

    Development Team vs. Traditional Teams

    Traditional Team Scrum Development Team
    ❌ Managed by others ✅ Self-organizing
    ❌ Siloed by function ✅ Cross-functional
    ❌ Individual accountability ✅ Team accountability
    ❌ Work assigned to individuals ✅ Team pulls work collaboratively

    👩‍🏫 The Scrum Master (SM)

    role scrum master
    The Scrum Master is responsible for promoting and supporting Scrum as defined in the Scrum Guide. Scrum Masters do this by helping everyone understand Scrum theory, practices, rules, and values.

    🧘 The Scrum Master as Servant-Leader

    The Scrum Master is a servant-leader for the Scrum Team. They help those outside the Scrum Team understand which interactions are helpful and which aren't. The Scrum Master helps everyone change these interactions to maximize the value created by the Scrum Team.

    Three Dimensions of Service

    👩‍💼 Service to Product Owner

    • Ensuring goals, scope, and product domain are understood by everyone
    • Helping the Scrum Team understand clear Product Backlog items
    • Understanding product planning in an empirical environment
    • Ensuring the Product Owner knows how to arrange the Product Backlog to maximize value
    • Understanding and practicing agility
    • Facilitating Scrum events as requested or needed

    👥 Service to Development Team

    • Coaching the Development Team in self-organization and cross-functionality
    • Helping the Development Team create high-value products
    • Removing impediments to the Development Team's progress
    • Facilitating Scrum events as requested or needed
    • Coaching in organizational environments where Scrum is not yet fully adopted

    🏢 Service to Organization

    • Leading and coaching the organization in Scrum adoption
    • Planning Scrum implementations within the organization
    • Helping employees and stakeholders understand Scrum and empirical product development
    • Causing change that increases productivity of the Scrum Team
    • Working with other Scrum Masters to increase effectiveness of Scrum application

    🛠️ Key Responsibilities

    Process Facilitation

    • Facilitate Scrum Events - Ensure all ceremonies are productive and within timeboxes
    • Protect the Team - Shield from external interruptions and distractions
    • Promote Self-Organization - Help the team make decisions independently
    • Enable Transparency - Make work visible and understood by all

    Coaching and Mentoring

    • Coach the Team - Guide in Scrum practices and principles
    • Mentor Individuals - Help team members grow in their roles
    • Facilitate Learning - Create opportunities for skill development
    • Challenge Status Quo - Encourage continuous improvement

    Impediment Removal

    • Remove Blockers - Address obstacles that slow the team down
    • Escalate When Needed - Get help from management for organizational impediments
    • Prevent Future Impediments - Work proactively to eliminate systemic issues

    What a Scrum Master is NOT

    • ❌ Not a Project Manager - Doesn't assign tasks or manage the team
    • ❌ Not the Team Lead - Team is self-organizing
    • ❌ Not a Task Master - Doesn't track who's doing what
    • ❌ Not the Product Owner - Doesn't make product decisions
    • ❌ Not a Secretary - Doesn't just take notes or schedule meetings
    • ❌ Not a Scrum Police - Doesn't enforce rules rigidly

    Skills and Qualities

    An effective Scrum Master demonstrates:

    • Servant Leadership - Puts the team's needs first
    • Coaching Ability - Guides without dictating
    • Facilitation Skills - Runs effective meetings and discussions
    • Emotional Intelligence - Understands team dynamics and individual needs
    • Conflict Resolution - Helps the team work through disagreements constructively
    • Change Management - Helps organization adopt Agile mindset
    • Systems Thinking - Sees how parts of the organization interconnect
    • Patience - Understands that change takes time

    The Scrum Master's Stance

    The Scrum Master wears many hats:

    • 🎭 Facilitator - Guides discussions and ceremonies
    • 🧑‍🏫 Coach - Teaches Scrum and Agile principles
    • 🛡️ Protector - Shields team from distractions
    • 🔧 Problem Solver - Removes impediments
    • 🌱 Change Agent - Drives organizational transformation
    • 👁️ Observer - Watches for improvement opportunities
    • 🤝 Collaborator - Works with other Scrum Masters

    🌍 Remote & Hybrid Scrum Teams

    modern practices remote work
    Modern Scrum teams often work remotely or in hybrid models. While Scrum principles remain the same, teams need to adapt their practices to ensure effective collaboration, communication, and transparency across distributed environments.
    🆕 2026 Reality: Remote and hybrid work are now the norm rather than the exception. Successful Scrum teams have learned how to maintain agility, transparency, and empiricism regardless of physical location.

    🏠 Remote Scrum Challenges & Solutions

    ⚠️ Challenge: Communication Barriers

    Reduced face-to-face interaction, time zone differences, misinterpretation of written communication

    ✅ Solutions:

    • Over-communicate and use video conferencing for all ceremonies
    • Establish core collaboration hours across time zones
    • Use emojis and clear written communication
    • Record important meetings for those in different time zones

    ⚠️ Challenge: Reduced Collaboration

    Lack of spontaneous discussions, difficulty with pair programming, reduced team cohesion

    ✅ Solutions:

    • Schedule regular virtual coffee breaks and social time
    • Use screen-sharing and collaboration tools (Miro, Mural)
    • Encourage always-on video during work hours (optional)
    • Pair program using VS Code Live Share or similar tools

    ⚠️ Challenge: Loss of Transparency

    Physical boards not visible, harder to see who's working on what, reduced visibility of impediments

    ✅ Solutions:

    • Use digital task boards (Jira, Azure DevOps, Trello)
    • Ensure all work is tracked and visible online
    • Update task status in real-time throughout the day
    • Use dashboards to visualize Sprint progress

    ⚠️ Challenge: Team Bonding

    Reduced informal interactions, harder to build trust, team feels disconnected

    ✅ Solutions:

    • Start meetings with personal check-ins
    • Organize virtual team-building activities
    • Consider periodic in-person meetups (quarterly)
    • Celebrate wins and milestones virtually

    📊 Scrum Metrics & Measurement

    metrics measurement
    While Scrum is empirical and outcome-focused, metrics help teams inspect and adapt. Modern Scrum teams use metrics not for command-and-control, but to understand their performance, identify improvements, and drive continuous learning.
    ⚠️ Important: Metrics should never be used to compare teams or individuals. They are for the team's own learning and improvement. Avoid vanity metrics that don't drive better outcomes.

    🎯 Evidence-Based Management (EBM)

    Framework Overview

    Evidence-Based Management is Scrum.org's framework for helping organizations to use empiricism to make strategic decisions and improve outcomes. It uses four Key Value Areas (KVAs) to measure and guide improvement.

    📈 Current Value (CV)

    Value delivered to customers today

    Revenue per employee, customer satisfaction, usage frequency, Net Promoter Score (NPS)

    🔮 Unrealized Value (UV)

    Potential value that could be realized

    Market gap, untapped customer segments, feature requests, opportunity cost

    ⏱️ Time to Market (T2M)

    Ability to deliver new value quickly

    Build time, release frequency, lead time, time to pivot, cycle time

    💪 Ability to Innovate (A2I)

    Effectiveness at delivering new capabilities

    Technical debt ratio, deployment pipeline speed, team morale, innovation rate

    📉 Common Scrum Metrics

    Velocity

    Definition: Total story points completed in a Sprint

    Purpose: Helps with Sprint Planning forecasting; shows trends over time

    ⚠️ Caution: Don't compare velocity across teams; don't use as productivity metric; don't pressure teams to increase velocity artificially

    Sprint Burndown

    Definition: Visual representation of remaining work (hours/points) in the Sprint over time

    Purpose: Shows if team is on track to complete Sprint commitment; helps identify blockers early

    Ideal Pattern: Downward trending line from Sprint start to zero by Sprint end

    Release Burndown

    Definition: Tracks remaining work (story points) across multiple Sprints toward a release

    Purpose: Forecasts release date; shows progress toward Product Goal

    Cycle Time & Lead Time

    Cycle Time: Time from when work starts until it's done (In Progress → Done)

    Lead Time: Time from when work is requested until it's delivered (Created → Done)

    Purpose: Measures flow efficiency; identifies bottlenecks; helps with forecasting

    Modern Focus: Used heavily in Kanban and continuous flow approaches

    Cumulative Flow Diagram (CFD)

    Definition: Stacked area chart showing work items in different states over time

    Purpose: Visualizes work in progress (WIP); identifies bottlenecks; shows flow consistency

    Ideal Pattern: Smooth, parallel bands with minimal bulging (indicates consistent flow)

    🎯 Outcome-Based Metrics (Modern Focus)

    Shift from outputs (features delivered) to outcomes (value realized)

    ❌ Output Metrics (Avoid Over-Reliance)

    • Story points completed
    • Number of features delivered
    • Lines of code written
    • Commits per developer

    ✅ Outcome Metrics (Preferred)

    🚫 Anti-Patterns to Avoid

    • Collecting metrics "because we always have"
    • Over-measuring and creating reporting burden

    • Purpose-driven: Only measure what helps you improve

    • Transparent: Share metrics with the team, not hide them in management reports

    Product Discovery is the process of understanding what to build before building it. Modern product teams use discovery techniques to reduce waste, validate assumptions, and ensure they're solving real user problems. Discovery runs continuously alongside delivery.

    Purpose: Create representative archetypes of target users to guide decisions

    Include: Demographics, goals, frustrations, behaviors, quote, photo

    Purpose: Visualize end-to-end user experience across touchpoints

    Elements: Stages, user actions, thoughts, emotions, pain points, opportunities

    Benefit: Identifies gaps and opportunities in current experience

    📊 Surveys & Quantitative Research

    Purpose: Gather data from larger sample to validate patterns from interviews

    Tools: Typeform, Google Forms, SurveyMonkey, Qualtrics

    Purpose: Watch users interact with prototype or product to identify issues

    📐 Prioritization Frameworks

    RICE Scoring

    Formula: (Reach × Impact × Confidence) / Effort = RICE Score

    • Reach: How many users affected per time period? (e.g., 1000 users/quarter)
    • Impact: How much will it impact each user? (Massive=3, High=2, Medium=1, Low=0.5, Minimal=0.25)
    • Confidence: How confident in estimates? (100%=High, 80%=Medium, 50%=Low)
    • Effort: Person-months required by all team members

    Higher score = higher priority

    Value vs. Effort Matrix

    Quick Wins
    High Value, Low Effort
    👉 Do First!
    Major Projects
    High Value, High Effort
    Plan carefully
    Fill-Ins
    Low Value, Low Effort
    Do if time permits
    Time Sinks
    Low Value, High Effort
    ❌ Avoid!

    Kano Model

    Categorizes features by their impact on customer satisfaction:

    • Must-Haves (Basic): Expected, cause dissatisfaction if missing (e.g., security)
    • Performance: More is better, linear satisfaction (e.g., speed)
    • Delighters: Unexpected, create delight, differentiate (e.g., AI assistant)
    • Indifferent: Users don't care either way (avoid building these!)

    📋 Backlog Refinement Best Practices

    • ✅ Ongoing Activity: Refinement is continuous, not a single meeting (≤10% of Sprint capacity)
    • ✅ Collaborative: PO, Developers, and Scrum Master participate
    • ✅ Right-Sized: Top items are small and ready; lower items can be larger and vaguer
    • ✅ 2-Sprint Horizon: Items for next 1-2 Sprints should be refined and ready
    • ✅ Definition of Ready: Criteria for items to enter Sprint Planning (e.g., acceptance criteria, estimated, dependencies identified)
    • ✅ Story Splitting: Break large items into smaller, valuable slices
    • ✅ Spike Stories: Time-boxed research for high-uncertainty items

    📝 Writing Effective User Stories

    user stories invest acceptance criteria agile

    Why User Stories Matter

    User stories are the backbone of agile delivery. They bridge the gap between business needs and technical implementation, ensuring everyone—product, design, engineering—shares the same understanding of what to build and why it matters.

    🎯 Core Principle:

    "User stories are not requirements—they are conversations." – Ron Jeffries

    A good story sparks discussion, not just documentation.

    Well-written stories ensure clarity, reduce rework, and keep the focus on customer value. They answer three critical questions:

    • Who is this for?
    • What do they need?
    • Why does it matter?

    What Makes a Great User Story?

    ✍️ Clear and Concise

    One feature or goal per story. Avoid multi-part epics disguised as stories.

    👤 Customer-Centric

    Written from the user's perspective. Focus on their needs, not technical tasks.

    ✅ Actionable

    Includes acceptance criteria for validation. Team knows what "done" looks like.

    The Standard User Story Format

    📋 Format Template

    As a [user role], I want [goal], so that [benefit/reason].

    Breaking It Down:

    • "As a..." – Who is the user? (Be specific: "new customer," "admin," "mobile user")
    • "I want..." – What capability do they need? (Focus on the goal, not implementation)
    • "So that..." – Why do they need it? (The value or outcome they're seeking)

    Example Transformation

    ❌ Bad Story

    "Add login functionality."

    Problems: No user, no context, no reason why, purely technical task

    ✅ Good Story

    "As a user, I want to log in securely so that I can access my account and personalized features."

    Why it works: Clear user, specific need, explicit benefit

    The INVEST Principle

    Coined by Bill Wake, INVEST is a checklist for evaluating user story quality. A good user story is:

    I – Independent

    Can be developed without dependencies on other stories. Reduces coordination overhead.

    Test: Can this story be built in any order?

    N – Negotiable

    Encourages collaboration, not rigid specs. Details emerge through conversation.

    Test: Is there room to discuss implementation details?

    V – Valuable

    Delivers business or customer value. Not just technical tasks.

    Test: Would a user or stakeholder care if this shipped?

    E – Estimable

    Can be sized for planning. Team has enough information to estimate effort.

    Test: Can the team give a rough estimate (even if uncertain)?

    S – Small

    Fits within a sprint. Large stories (epics) should be broken down.

    Test: Can this be completed in 1-2 weeks?

    T – Testable

    Has clear acceptance criteria. You can verify when it's done.

    Test: Can QA or stakeholders validate success?

    Acceptance Criteria: The Key to Testability

    Acceptance Criteria (AC) define what "done" looks like. They're the contract between product and engineering.

    Good Acceptance Criteria:

    • Specific: No ambiguity about what should happen
    • Testable: Can be verified manually or automated
    • Agreed Upon: Product, design, and engineering align before development starts
    • Focused on Behavior: Describe outcomes, not implementation

    Formats for Writing Acceptance Criteria

    1. Given-When-Then (Gherkin Format)

    Popular in Behavior-Driven Development (BDD). Focuses on scenarios and context.

    📝 Example: Login Story

    Story:

    "As a user, I want to log in securely so that I can access my account."

    Acceptance Criteria:

    • Given I am on the login page
    • When I enter a valid email and password and click "Login"
    • Then I should be redirected to my dashboard
    • Given I enter an invalid email or password
    • When I click "Login"
    • Then I should see an error message: "Invalid credentials"

    2. Checklist Format

    Simple bullet points outlining expected behaviors.

    ✅ Example: Password Reset Story

    Story:

    "As a user, I want to reset my password so that I can regain access if I forget it."

    Acceptance Criteria:

    • Login page has a "Forgot Password?" link
    • Clicking link opens password reset form
    • User enters email address and submits
    • System sends password reset link to email
    • Password reset link expires after 24 hours
    • New password must meet security requirements (8+ chars, 1 number, 1 special char)
    • After reset, user can log in with new password

    Complete Example with Acceptance Criteria

    📋 Full Story Example

    User Story:

    "As a user, I want to log in securely so that I can access my account and personalized features."

    Acceptance Criteria:

    • Login requires email and password
    • Password must meet security standards (8+ characters, 1 uppercase, 1 number)
    • Error message shown for invalid credentials
    • Successful login redirects to user dashboard
    • "Remember Me" option keeps user logged in for 30 days
    • Account locked after 5 failed login attempts

    Definition of Done:

    • Code reviewed and merged
    • Unit tests written (90%+ coverage)
    • Manual QA passed
    • Security review completed
    • Documentation updated

    Common User Story Pitfalls

    ⚠️ Avoid These Mistakes

    1. Technical Tasks Disguised as Stories: "As a developer, I want to refactor the API..." (Not user-facing value)
    2. No "So That" Clause: Missing the "why"—why does the user need this?
    3. Too Large (Epics): Stories that take multiple sprints to complete
    4. No Acceptance Criteria: Team has no shared definition of "done"
    5. Prescriptive Implementation: Telling engineers exactly how to build it
    6. Multiple User Roles: "As a user and admin..." (Split into separate stories)
    7. Vague Language: "Improve performance" or "Make it better" (Not measurable)

    Example: Refactoring a Bad Story

    Before (Bad) After (Good)

    "Improve the dashboard."

    Problems: Vague, no user, no success criteria

    "As a sales manager, I want to see real-time revenue metrics on my dashboard so that I can make informed decisions during client calls."

    AC: Dashboard updates every 5 seconds, displays total revenue, top 5 deals, win rate

    Story Size: Epics vs. Stories vs. Tasks

    Level Description Timeline Example
    Epic Large body of work spanning multiple sprints 1-3 months "Build a mobile app"
    Story User-facing feature or capability that fits in a sprint 1-2 weeks "As a user, I want to log in via mobile"
    Task Technical subtask within a story Hours to days "Set up OAuth integration"

    Breaking Down Large Stories (Story Splitting)

    If a story is too big, split it using these techniques:

    1. Split by User Role

    • Original: "As a user, I want to manage my profile"
    • Split: "As a customer, I want to update my email" + "As an admin, I want to view all user profiles"

    2. Split by Workflow Steps

    • Original: "As a user, I want to complete checkout"
    • Split: "Add to cart" + "Enter shipping info" + "Process payment" + "Confirm order"

    3. Split by Business Rules

    • Original: "As a user, I want discounts applied"
    • Split: "Apply coupon codes" + "Apply volume discounts" + "Apply loyalty discounts"

    4. Split by Happy Path vs. Edge Cases

    • Sprint 1: "As a user, I want to log in successfully (happy path)"
    • Sprint 2: "As a user, I want error handling for failed login attempts"

    User Story Estimation

    Teams estimate story size using:

    • Story Points: Relative sizing (Fibonacci: 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13...)
    • T-Shirt Sizes: S, M, L, XL
    • Time-Based: Hours or days (less common in agile)

    💡 Story Point Guidelines

    • 1-2 points: Simple, well-understood change
    • 3-5 points: Moderate complexity, some unknowns
    • 8 points: Complex, significant effort, consider splitting
    • 13+ points: Epic—must be broken down before sprint planning

    Tools for Managing User Stories

    • Jira, Azure DevOps: Full-featured backlog management and sprint planning
    • Linear, Shortcut: Lightweight, modern story tracking
    • Trello, Asana: Kanban-style boards for simple story management
    • Pivotal Tracker: Story-centric agile planning
    • GitHub Issues, GitLab Issues: Developer-friendly story tracking

    Best Practices for Writing User Stories

    • 📝 Collaborate: Write stories with the team, not for the team
    • 🎯 Focus on Value: Every story should deliver customer or business value
    • 🔄 Refine Regularly: Dedicate time each sprint to backlog refinement
    • ✅ Include Acceptance Criteria: No story is ready without clear AC
    • 🧪 Think Testability: If you can't test it, you can't build it
    • 📏 Keep It Small: Split large stories into sprint-sized chunks
    • 🗣️ Use Simple Language: Avoid jargon, write for everyone on the team
    • 👥 Involve QA Early: QA can spot gaps in acceptance criteria

    The 3 C's of User Stories

    Introduced by Ron Jeffries, co-creator of Extreme Programming (XP):

    📇 Card

    Written on an index card (or digital card). Just enough info to spark conversation.

    💬 Conversation

    Stories are placeholders for discussion. Details emerge through collaboration.

    ✅ Confirmation

    Acceptance criteria confirm the story is complete and working as intended.

    Shifting Your Thinking

    Instead of writing:

    "Build a search feature."

    Ask:

    "Who needs search? What are they trying to accomplish? Why can't they do it today?"

    💡 Tip for the Week:

    Before finalizing a story, check:

    • Does it follow INVEST? (Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable)
    • Is the user benefit clear? (Can you explain the "so that" part?)
    • Are acceptance criteria testable? (Can QA verify when it's done?)

    If you can't check all three boxes, the story isn't ready for development.

    📈 Scaling Scrum

    scaling enterprise
    When products grow beyond a single Scrum Team, organizations need approaches to coordinate multiple teams working on the same product. Scaling frameworks provide patterns and practices for enterprise agility while maintaining Scrum's core principles.
    ⚠️ Important: Don't scale Scrum unless you need to. A single team is simpler, faster, and more efficient. Only scale when product complexity truly requires multiple teams. "The best way to scale is not to scale."

    🔍 When to Scale Scrum

    Consider scaling when:

    • Product complexity exceeds single team capacity
    • Multiple skill sets required that one team can't have
    • Time-to-market demands parallel work
    • Geographic distribution necessitates multiple locations

    Before scaling, try:

    • Optimizing single team's skills and processes
    • Reducing product complexity
    • Improving engineering practices to increase throughput

    ⚖️ Comparison of Scaling Frameworks

    1. Scrum@Scale (Scrum of Scrums)

    Origin: Created by Jeff Sutherland (Scrum Guide co-creator)

    Philosophy: Minimum viable bureaucracy; scales Scrum naturally by replicating Scrum patterns

    Structure:

    • Scrum of Scrums (SoS): Representatives from each team meet to coordinate
    • Executive Action Team (EAT): Leadership team providing strategic direction
    • Scrum of Scrum of Scrums (SoSoS): For very large organizations
    • Executive MetaScrum (EMS): Product Owners coordinate product strategy

    Best For: Organizations wanting minimal framework overhead

    Certification: Scrum@Scale Practitioner

    2. SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework)

    Origin: Created by Dean Leffingwell

    Philosophy: Comprehensive, prescriptive framework for enterprise-wide agility

    Structure:

    • Essential SAFe: Basics for a single Agile Release Train (ART)
    • Portfolio SAFe: Aligns strategy with execution
    • Large Solution SAFe: For very large solutions
    • Full SAFe: Complete framework for large enterprises
    • PI Planning: 2-day event every 8-12 weeks for synchronization
    • ART (Agile Release Train): 50-125 people organized around value stream

    Best For: Large enterprises needing governance and portfolio management

    Certifications: SA (SAFe Agilist), SSM (SAFe Scrum Master), POPM (Product Owner/Product Manager)

    Note: Most comprehensive but also most heavyweight

    Philosophy: "More with LeSS" - simplify and descale; organizational change focused

    🛠️ Key Scaling Practices (Framework-Agnostic)

    🏗️ Architecture & Technical Practices

    • Modular Architecture: Microservices or modular monolith to minimize dependencies
    • Continuous Integration: Frequent integration of all teams' work
    • Shared Definition of Done: Common quality standards across teams
    • API-First Design: Teams develop contracts before implementation
    • Automated Testing: Critical at scale - manual testing doesn't scale

    👥 Team Structure

    • Feature Teams > Component Teams: Cross-functional teams delivering end-to-end features
    • Stable Teams: Keep teams together long-term; don't shuffle members
    • Minimize Dependencies: Design team boundaries to reduce coordination needs
    • Shared Team Members (Sparingly): Avoid but sometimes necessary for specialty skills

    🔄 Coordination Mechanisms

    • Scrum of Scrums: Regular sync between team representatives (daily or few times/week)
    • Communities of Practice: Groups sharing knowledge across teams (e.g., testing CoP, frontend CoP)
    • Architectural Runway: Advance technical work to support future features
    • Cross-Team Refinement: Joint backlog refinement when items span teams
    • Big Room Planning: Periodic in-person planning (PI Planning in SAFe)
    • Integration Points: Regular demonstration of integrated work

    ⚠️ Common Scaling Anti-Patterns

    • ❌ Adding process instead of removing barriers: More meetings, more roles, more artifacts
    • ❌ Component teams instead of feature teams: Creates handoffs and delays
    • ❌ Scaling before mastering single-team Scrum: Amplifies dysfunction
    • ❌ Ignoring technical practices: Without CI/CD, testing, architecture - teams can't integrate
    • ❌ Different Sprint lengths across teams: Makes synchronization difficult
    • ❌ Multiple Product Owners for one product: Lack of unified vision and priorities
    • ❌ Over-coordination: Too many sync meetings reduce team productivity

    🎯 Descaling: The Better Alternative

    Instead of scaling, consider descaling the organization:

    • ✅ Simplify the product: Remove unused features (most features are rarely used)
    • ✅ Modular architecture: Allow teams to work independently
    • ✅ Cross-train team members: Increase team capabilities
    • ✅ Automate: Reduce manual work that requires more people
    • ✅ Split products: Multiple small products with dedicated teams beats one huge product
    • ✅ Remove organizational silos: Eliminate handoffs between Dev, QA, Ops, Security

    💚 Team Health & Wellbeing

    team health psychological safety
    Sustainable high performance requires healthy teams. Psychological safety, work-life balance, and team dynamics are as critical to success as technical practices. Scrum emphasizes people first.
    🎯 Remember: Agile values "Individuals and interactions over processes and tools." A healthy team outperforms a burned-out team with perfect processes every time.

    🛡️ Psychological Safety

    Definition: A belief that one won't be punished or humiliated for speaking up with ideas, questions, concerns, or mistakes.

    Coined by Amy Edmondson (Harvard). Google's Project Aristotle found it the #1 predictor of team effectiveness.

    Five Conditions for Psychological Safety

    1. Frame Work as Learning

    Position the team as solving problems together, not executing perfectly. Emphasize experiments and learning.

    2. Acknowledge Fallibility

    Leaders model vulnerability. Say "I don't know" or "I made a mistake" to set the tone.

    3. Model Curiosity

    Ask lots of questions. Show genuine interest in others' perspectives. "What do you think?" "Help me understand..."

    4. Create Space for All Voices

    Ensure everyone speaks in meetings. Actively invite quieter members to share. Limit dominant voices.

    5. Respond Productively

    When mistakes happen, focus on learning, not blame. "What can we learn?" not "Who did this?"

    🔍 Measuring Psychological Safety

    • Anonymous surveys (Edmondson's 7-item scale)
    • Retrospective discussions: "Do we feel safe speaking up?"
    • Observation: Do all team members contribute in meetings?
    • Mistake handling: Are mistakes discussed openly or hidden?
    • Disagreement frequency: Healthy teams disagree constructively

    ⚖️ Sustainable Pace & Avoiding Burnout

    ⚠️ XP Principle: "The team must work at a pace they can sustain indefinitely." Overtime is a sign of planning failure, not dedication.

    Warning Signs of Burnout

    😰 Emotional Signs

    • Irritability or cynicism
    • Detachment from work
    • Reduced sense of accomplishment
    • Anxiety or depression

    🏃 Physical Signs

    • Chronic fatigue
    • Insomnia or sleep issues
    • Physical health problems
    • Reduced immunity

    💼 Performance Signs

    • Decreased productivity
    • Missing deadlines
    • Quality decline
    • Withdrawing from team

    🌿 Practices for Sustainable Pace

    • ✅ 40-hour work week as baseline: Occasional overtime acceptable, but chronic overtime indicates systemic problems
    • ✅ Respect time off: No emails/Slack outside working hours; honor vacation time
    • ✅ Realistic Sprint commitments: Use historical velocity; don't overcommit
    • ✅ Built-in slack time: Reserve 10-20% capacity for unplanned work, learning, technical debt
    • ✅ Encourage breaks: Pomodoro technique, walk breaks, genuine lunch breaks
    • ✅ Balance types of work: Mix challenging and routine tasks
    • ✅ Rotation of on-call/support duties: Distribute stressful responsibilities
    • ✅ Address technical debt regularly: Prevents future stress

    🃏 Estimation in Scrum

    estimation story points planning poker
    Estimation in Scrum is about building shared understanding, not precise prediction. Teams estimate to facilitate planning, expose unknowns, and drive conversation — not to create deadlines or commitments.
    💡 Key Insight: The goal of estimation is the conversation it generates, not the number it produces. When teams disagree on an estimate, it reveals hidden complexity, different assumptions, or missing information — all valuable discoveries.

    🎯 Why Estimate?

    📅 Sprint Planning

    Helps the team decide how much work fits in a Sprint based on past velocity.

    📊 Backlog Prioritization

    Enables the Product Owner to weigh effort against value when ordering the backlog.

    🔍 Identify Complexity

    Large estimates signal unclear requirements, dependencies, or risks — prompting refinement.

    🤝 Build Shared Understanding

    The discussion during estimation ensures everyone understands the work the same way.

    📈 Forecast Releases

    Aggregated estimates and velocity allow rough projections of when epics or features will ship.

    📏 Story Points

    What Are Story Points?

    Story points are a unit of measure for expressing the relative effort of implementing a user story. They combine complexity, uncertainty, and effort into a single abstract number — deliberately avoiding hours to prevent the trap of false precision.

    📐 Key Properties of Story Points

    ✅ Relative, Not Absolute

    Points measure size relative to other stories, not calendar time. A 3-point story is roughly 3× the effort of a 1-point story — regardless of who does it or when.

    ✅ Team-Specific

    A 5-point story for Team A is not comparable to a 5-point story for Team B. Points are calibrated within a single team context and should never be compared across teams.

    ✅ Combines Three Factors

    Complexity (how hard is this?), Effort (how much work?), and Uncertainty (how much do we know?) all influence a story point estimate.

    ✅ Improves Over Time

    As teams build a reference library of estimated stories, future estimates become faster and more consistent. Calibration is a long-term investment.

    🔢 The Fibonacci Sequence

    Most teams use a modified Fibonacci scale for story point values:

    1 2 3 5 8 13 21 ?

    Why Fibonacci? The growing gaps between numbers reflect the natural increase in uncertainty as stories get larger. It's hard to distinguish a 6 from a 7, but 5 vs. 8 is a meaningful difference worth debating.

    📊 Story Point Reference Guide

    Points What It Means Typical Examples Action
    1 Trivial. Fully understood, minimal effort, no unknowns. Update a label, fix a typo, change a config value ✅ Ready to sprint
    2 Small. Well-understood with minor complexity. Add a field to a form, simple CRUD endpoint ✅ Ready to sprint
    3 Moderate. Some complexity or minor unknowns. New API integration (documented), UI component with logic ✅ Ready to sprint
    5 Complex. Multiple components affected, noticeable effort. New feature with backend + frontend, role-based permissions ✅ Ensure AC is clear
    8 Very complex. Significant effort or meaningful unknowns. Boundary of sprintable. New service integration, data migration, complex business logic ⚠️ Consider splitting
    13 Too large for a single sprint. Very high complexity or uncertainty. Full feature rework, architectural change, brand new domain 🔴 Must be split
    21+ Epic. Not a user story — requires decomposition before estimation. Entire module, product integration 🔴 Split immediately
    ? Unknown. Team lacks enough information to estimate. Any story where requirements are unclear 🔴 Needs refinement first

    🃏 Planning Poker

    What Is Planning Poker?

    Planning Poker (also called Scrum Poker) is a consensus-based estimation technique invented by James Grenning and popularized by Mike Cohn. It uses gamified voting cards to prevent anchoring bias and surface diverse perspectives simultaneously.

    🎮 How Planning Poker Works

    Step 1: Present the Story

    The Product Owner reads the user story aloud and answers clarifying questions. No one reveals their estimate yet.

    Step 2: Silent Individual Estimation

    Each team member privately selects a card representing their estimate. This prevents anchoring — no one influences others before voting.

    Step 3: Simultaneous Reveal

    All cards are flipped at the same time. This is the critical moment — divergent estimates signal different assumptions or missing information.

    Step 4: Discuss Divergence

    The highest and lowest estimators explain their reasoning. No one is wrong — different perspectives reveal complexity. Focus on facts and risks, not persuasion.

    Step 5: Re-vote Until Consensus

    Repeat steps 2–4 until the team converges. Consensus does not mean unanimity — it means everyone can live with the estimate. Two rounds is typically enough.

    Step 6: Record and Move On

    Record the agreed estimate in the backlog tool and move to the next story. Time-box each item to 5–10 minutes to keep momentum.

    🔥 Why Simultaneous Reveal Matters

    ❌ Without Simultaneous Reveal (Anchoring Bias)

    If the lead developer says "this is a 5" first, everyone else unconsciously adjusts toward 5, suppressing their true assessment. The result is false consensus.

    ✅ With Simultaneous Reveal

    Everyone commits independently. When a senior dev plays 3 and a junior dev plays 13, it surfaces that they have fundamentally different understandings — a critical conversation to have before coding starts.

    👕 T-Shirt Sizing

    🪣 Bucket System

    The bucket system is designed for estimating large backlogs quickly (20–500+ items). Stories are placed into labeled "buckets" on a wall or virtual board, sorted by relative size.

    📈 Velocity & Using Estimates

    Team Velocity

    Velocity is the average number of story points a team completes per Sprint. It's measured over 3–5 sprints and used to predict how much work fits in the next sprint — never as a performance target.

    • Sprint 1: 28 pts → Sprint 2: 34 pts → Sprint 3: 31 pts → Average velocity ≈ 31 pts
    • Use the average to set a realistic Sprint Goal capacity
    • Velocity naturally stabilizes after 3–4 sprints as estimates and team calibrate
    • A sudden velocity change is a signal — investigate the cause (scope creep, tech debt, team change)
    ⚠️ Velocity Anti-Patterns: Never compare velocity across teams. Never use velocity as a performance KPI. Never pressure teams to increase velocity artificially — it leads to inflated estimates and eroded trust.

    ✅ Estimation Best Practices

    • ✅ Estimate during refinement, not during Sprint Planning — stories should be estimated before they enter a Sprint
    • ✅ Use reference stories — keep 1–2 well-known "anchor" stories at each size to calibrate the team
    • ✅ Time-box discussions — if consensus isn't reached in two rounds, go with the higher estimate or mark as a spike
    • ✅ Involve the whole team — developers, testers, and designers all have unique perspectives on effort
    • ✅ Re-estimate when scope changes — a story that grows in scope during a Sprint should be flagged
    • ✅ Treat 0 as valid — if a story is truly trivial, a 0 or 1 is acceptable
    • ✅ Track actuals vs. estimates — retrospectively reviewing large deviations improves future accuracy

    🚫 Estimation Anti-Patterns

    ⚠️ Common Pitfalls to Avoid

    • Estimating in hours: Creates false precision and micro-management pressure. Use relative points.
    • Anchoring: One person reveals their estimate first, biasing everyone else. Always reveal simultaneously.
    • HiPPO effect: The Highest Paid Person's Opinion silences dissent. Estimation must be democratic.
    • Skipping unknowns: Never estimate a story the team doesn't understand — use a spike instead.
    • Treating estimates as commitments: Estimates are forecasts, not promises. Plans change.
    • Comparing team velocities: Different teams, different contexts. Velocity is internal only.
    • Inflating estimates as buffer: Padding estimates to avoid pressure destroys forecast accuracy over time.

    🔬 Spikes: When You Can't Estimate

    🧪 What Is a Spike?

    A spike is a time-boxed research or investigation task used when a story has too much uncertainty to estimate. The output is knowledge (a technical decision, a proof of concept, or clarified requirements) — not production code.

    • Example: "Spike: Investigate OAuth 2.0 integration with legacy SSO system (max 4 hours)"
    • Spikes have a fixed time box — stop when time is up, report findings, then estimate the real story
    • Spikes should be small (a few hours to 1 day) — if bigger, the problem needs further decomposition

    📦 Scrum Artifacts

    artifacts work products
    Scrum's artifacts represent work or value to provide transparency and opportunities for inspection and adaptation. Artifacts defined by Scrum are specifically designed to maximize transparency of key information so that everybody has the same understanding of the artifact.

    📍 The Three Core Artifacts

    📚 Product Backlog

    An ordered list of everything that is known to be needed in the product. Single source of requirements for any changes to be made to the product.

    📋 Sprint Backlog

    The set of Product Backlog items selected for the Sprint, plus a plan for delivering the product Increment and realizing the Sprint Goal.

    🚀 Increment

    The sum of all Product Backlog items completed during a Sprint and the value of all increments of all previous Sprints. Must be in usable condition.

    Key Principles of Artifacts

    • 🔍 Transparency - Artifacts must be transparent to all stakeholders
    • 📊 Inspection - Regularly inspected to detect undesirable variances
    • 🔧 Adaptation - Adjusted when inspection reveals issues
    • 🎯 Shared Understanding - Everyone has the same understanding of the artifact
    • 📝 Living Documents - Continuously updated throughout the project

    Artifact Commitment

    Each artifact contains a commitment to ensure it provides information that enhances transparency and focus against which progress can be measured:

    • Product Backlog → Commitment: Product Goal
    • Sprint Backlog → Commitment: Sprint Goal
    • Increment → Commitment: Definition of Done

    ⚠️ Important

    Artifacts and their commitments exist to reinforce empiricism and the Scrum values for the Scrum Team and their stakeholders. They are designed to maximize transparency of key information so that everyone has the same understanding.

    🗃 Product Backlog

    artifact backlog
    The Product Backlog is an ordered list of everything that is known to be needed in the product. It is the single source of requirements for any changes to be made to the product.

    📍 Key Characteristics

    🔢 Ordered List

    Items are ordered by priority, with highest value items at the top. Order is determined by the Product Owner based on value, risk, dependencies, and other factors.

    🔄 Dynamic

    Never complete. Constantly evolves as the product and environment evolve. It is a living artifact that changes to identify what the product needs.

    🎯 Single Source

    The only source of requirements for any changes to be made to the product. If it's not in the Product Backlog, it won't be worked on.

    📝 What's in a Product Backlog?

    • User Stories - Features described from user perspective
    • Features - Larger pieces of functionality
    • Enhancements - Improvements to existing features
    • Bug Fixes - Defects that need to be resolved
    • Technical Debt - Code improvements and refactoring
    • Knowledge Acquisition - Research and spikes

    👑 Product Owner Responsibilities

    The Product Owner is responsible for the Product Backlog, including its content, availability, and ordering. This includes:

    • Clearly expressing Product Backlog items
    • Ordering items to best achieve goals and missions
    • Optimizing the value of work the Development Team performs
    • Ensuring visibility, transparency, and clarity of the Product Backlog
    • Ensuring the Development Team understands items to level needed

    📈 Product Backlog Refinement

    Product Backlog refinement is the act of adding detail, estimates, and order to items in the Product Backlog. This is an ongoing process in which the Product Owner and Development Team collaborate on the details of Product Backlog items.

    Refinement Activities

    • ✅ Breaking down large items into smaller ones
    • ✅ Adding acceptance criteria
    • ✅ Estimating effort (story points)
    • ✅ Clarifying requirements
    • ✅ Re-ordering based on new information
    • ✅ Removing obsolete items

    🎯 Definition of Ready (DoR)

    🟢 Definition of Ready (DoR)

    The Definition of Ready is a set of criteria that a Product Backlog item must satisfy before the team can pull it into a Sprint. It ensures that Sprint Planning is productive and that the team can commit to delivering an item within the Sprint.

    Unlike the Definition of Done (which is an official Scrum artifact commitment), DoR is a widely adopted practice — not mandated by the Scrum Guide — but used by most teams to prevent ambiguous or under-prepared work from entering a Sprint.

    📝 Clearly Defined
    • User story written in agreed format
    • Business value is clear
    • Acceptance criteria defined
    📊 Estimated & Sized
    • Story points assigned by team
    • Small enough to complete in a Sprint
    • Technical approach understood
    🔗 Dependencies Resolved
    • External dependencies identified
    • Blockers removed or mitigated
    • Required resources available
    ✅ Team Acknowledged
    • Reviewed in refinement session
    • Team has no blocking questions
    • Design/UX assets attached (if needed)

    ⚖️ DoR vs. DoD — Key Differences

    Definition of Ready (DoR) Definition of Done (DoD)
    Purpose Item is ready to start Item is ready to ship
    Applied at Sprint Planning entry Sprint completion / release
    Scrum Guide Not official — widely practiced Official artifact commitment
    Owned by Product Owner + Team Scrum Team (Developers)

    🎯 Product Goal (Commitment)

    Product Goal

    The Product Goal describes a future state of the product which serves as a target for the Scrum Team to plan against. The Product Goal is in the Product Backlog. The rest of the Product Backlog emerges to define "what" will fulfill the Product Goal.

    The Product Goal is the long-term objective for the Scrum Team. They must fulfill (or abandon) one objective before taking on the next.

    Product Backlog Item Attributes

    📝 Description

    Clear statement of what needs to be done

    🔢 Order/Priority

    Relative importance in the backlog

    📊 Estimate

    Size or effort (story points)

    🎯 Value

    Business value or benefit

    ✅ Acceptance Criteria

    Conditions for completion

    Best Practices

    • ✅ Keep items at the top of the backlog well-refined and ready
    • ✅ Use INVEST criteria for user stories (Independent, Negotiable, Valuable, Estimable, Small, Testable)
    • ✅ Include acceptance criteria for each item
    • ✅ Regularly groom and refine the backlog
    • ✅ Make the backlog visible to all stakeholders
    • ✅ Keep lower priority items less detailed (just-in-time refinement)
    • ✅ Limit work in progress by having a reasonable backlog size

    🗂 Sprint Backlog

    artifact sprint
    The Sprint Backlog is the set of Product Backlog items selected for the Sprint, plus a plan for delivering the product Increment and realizing the Sprint Goal. It is a forecast by the Development Team about what functionality will be in the next Increment and the work needed to deliver that functionality.

    📍 Key Characteristics

    🎯 Sprint-Specific

    Created during Sprint Planning. Contains only the work planned for the current Sprint. A new Sprint Backlog is created for each Sprint.

    👥 Team Owned

    Owned by the Development Team. Only the Development Team can change its Sprint Backlog during a Sprint. The Sprint Backlog is a highly visible, real-time picture of the work.

    🔄 Flexible

    Emerges and evolves during the Sprint. As new work is required, the Development Team adds it to the Sprint Backlog. Work may be clarified and re-estimated.

    📝 What's in the Sprint Backlog?

    📚 Product Backlog Items

    Selected user stories, features, or work items from the Product Backlog that the team commits to completing in this Sprint.

    ✅ Tasks

    Detailed tasks necessary to deliver the selected Product Backlog items. Tasks are created by decomposing the backlog items.

    ⛳ Sprint Goal

    An objective set for the Sprint that provides guidance to the team on why it is building the Increment.

    🛠️ How Sprint Backlog is Created

    1. Sprint Planning Meeting - Development Team selects items from Product Backlog
    2. Team Discussion - Team discusses how to accomplish the work
    3. Task Breakdown - Items are decomposed into smaller tasks (often hours)
    4. Capacity Planning - Team ensures work fits within Sprint capacity
    5. Commitment - Team commits to delivering the selected items

    📈 Monitoring Progress

    📉 Burndown Chart

    Visualizes remaining work over time during the Sprint. Shows if the team is on track to complete all committed work.

    📊 Task Board

    Visual representation of work items (To Do, In Progress, Done). Updated during Daily Standup to show real-time progress.

    ⏱️ Daily Scrum

    15-minute daily meeting where team inspects progress and adapts the Sprint Backlog as necessary.

    ⛳ Sprint Goal (Commitment)

    Sprint Goal

    The Sprint Goal is an objective set for the Sprint that can be met through the implementation of the Product Backlog. It provides guidance to the Development Team on why it is building the Increment.

    The Sprint Goal gives the Development Team some flexibility regarding the functionality implemented within the Sprint. The selected Product Backlog items deliver one coherent function, which can be the Sprint Goal.

    Development Team Authority

    Only the Development Team can change its Sprint Backlog during a Sprint.

    • Add new tasks as they discover more work is needed
    • Remove tasks that are no longer necessary
    • Update task estimates as they learn more
    • Re-negotiate scope with Product Owner if Sprint Goal is at risk

    Sprint Backlog vs Product Backlog

    Product Backlog Sprint Backlog
    Owned by Product Owner Owned by Development Team
    Long-term (product lifetime) Short-term (one Sprint)
    Contains all known work Contains selected work for Sprint
    Ordered by value Work breakdown with tasks

    Best Practices

    • ✅ Make it visible - Use a task board that everyone can see
    • ✅ Update daily - Keep it current during Daily Scrum
    • ✅ Break items into small tasks - Typically 1-8 hours each
    • ✅ Track remaining work - Focus on what's left, not what's done
    • ✅ Protect the Sprint Goal - Don't add unplanned work unless critical
    • ✅ Be transparent - Make progress visible to all stakeholders

    📦 Increment

    artifact done
    The Increment is the sum of all the Product Backlog items completed during a Sprint and the value of the increments of all previous Sprints. At the end of a Sprint, the new Increment must be "Done", which means it must be in useable condition and meet the Scrum Team's Definition of Done.

    📍 Key Characteristics

    ✅ "Done" and Useable

    The Increment must be in useable condition regardless of whether the Product Owner decides to release it. It meets the Scrum Team's Definition of Done.

    📦 Cumulative

    The Increment is cumulative - all previous Increments must be thoroughly tested, ensuring that all Increments work together.

    🚀 Potentially Releasable

    Must be in a state where it could be released to users if the Product Owner chooses to do so. No additional work is required.

    ✅ Definition of Done (Commitment)

    ✅ Definition of Done (DoD)

    The Definition of Done is a formal description of the state of the Increment when it meets the quality measures required for the product.

    The moment a Product Backlog item meets the Definition of Done, an Increment is born. The Definition of Done creates transparency by providing everyone a shared understanding of what work was completed as part of the Increment.

    📝 What Might Be in Definition of Done?

    📝 Code Complete
    • Code reviewed
    • Merged to main branch
    • Follows coding standards
    🧪 Testing
    • Unit tests pass
    • Integration tests pass
    • Acceptance criteria met
    📚 Documentation
    • User documentation updated
    • Technical docs updated
    • Release notes prepared
    🚀 Deployment Ready
    • Deployed to staging
    • Performance tested
    • Security reviewed

    📈 Building the Increment

    1. Sprint Planning - Team selects Product Backlog items for the Sprint
    2. Development Work - Team builds features according to acceptance criteria
    3. Continuous Integration - Code is integrated frequently (ideally daily)
    4. Testing - Work is tested against Definition of Done
    5. Sprint Review - Increment is demonstrated to stakeholders
    6. Potentially Release - Product Owner decides whether to release

    Multiple Increments per Sprint

    Multiple Increments may be created within a Sprint. The sum of the Increments is presented at the Sprint Review thus supporting empiricism. However, an Increment may be delivered to stakeholders prior to the end of the Sprint.

    Some teams practice Continuous Deployment, where Increments are released multiple times per day.

    Increment vs Sprint

    • 📦 Increment is the artifact - the actual working product
    • ⏱️ Sprint is the timebox - the container event during which work is done
    • ✅ Each Sprint must produce at least one Increment
    • 🚀 The Increment must be "Done" and potentially releasable

    Why "Done" Matters

    • 🔍 Transparency - Everyone knows what "complete" means
    • 🎯 Quality - Ensures consistent quality standards
    • ⏱️ No Surprises - No hidden work or technical debt
    • 🚀 Releasability - Product Owner can release anytime
    • 📊 Velocity Accuracy - Only "Done" work counts toward velocity
    • 🤝 Trust - Stakeholders trust the team's commitments

    ⚠️ Warning: "Almost Done" is Not Done

    If a Product Backlog item does not meet the Definition of Done, it cannot be released or even presented at the Sprint Review. Instead, it returns to the Product Backlog for future consideration.

    Best Practices

    • ✅ Define "Done" clearly and make it visible to everyone
    • ✅ Ensure Definition of Done is realistic and achievable within Sprint
    • ✅ Include all necessary quality gates (testing, review, documentation)
    • ✅ Evolve Definition of Done over time as team matures
    • ✅ Never compromise on Definition of Done to "finish" work
    • ✅ Automate as much as possible (CI/CD, testing, deployment)
    • ✅ Get stakeholder agreement on Definition of Done

    📋 Kanban Introduction

    intro origins concepts
    The Kanban Method is a pull-based, flow-oriented approach to managing knowledge work. Adapted from Toyota's manufacturing system by David J. Anderson in 2007, it has evolved into a complete management method for service delivery — far beyond just putting cards on a board.

    📜 Origins & History

    🏭 1940s — Toyota Production System

    Taiichi Ohno at Toyota developed the original kanban (signboard) system to control inventory in a manufacturing supply chain. Cards signaled when to replenish materials, creating a pull-based just-in-time flow.

    💻 2004–2007 — Software Adaptation

    David J. Anderson adapted Toyota's kanban concepts for software at Microsoft and Corbis. His 2007 work formalized the Kanban Method (capital K) as a distinct approach for managing knowledge work and service delivery.

    🎓 2010 — Kanban Book

    Anderson published "Kanban: Successful Evolutionary Change for Your Technology Business", defining the Kanban Method with its principles and 6 core practices that teams worldwide follow today.

    🌐 2019+ — Professional Scrum with Kanban

    Scrum.org introduced Professional Scrum with Kanban (PSK), formalizing how Kanban practices can be combined with Scrum to improve flow without abandoning the Scrum framework.

    🎯 What is "Kanban"?

    🎴 lowercase kanban

    The visual signal card (literally "signboard" in Japanese) that triggers action. In a workflow, it's the ticket, card, or work item that moves through stages. Used in Toyota's original manufacturing system.

    🎴 Capital-K Kanban Method

    The complete management framework for service delivery and knowledge work. It includes foundational principles, core practices, feedback cadences, and flow metrics. Goes far beyond just using cards on a board.

    🔄 Pull vs Push Systems

    ❌ Push System (Traditional)

    • Work is pushed onto teams by managers or schedules
    • Teams must accept all incoming work regardless of capacity
    • Creates overloading, context switching, and long queues
    • Reduces quality due to multitasking pressure
    • Bottlenecks stay invisible until it's too late

    ✅ Pull System (Kanban)

    • Work is pulled by team members when they have capacity
    • WIP limits prevent overloading — teams can say "not yet"
    • Teams focus on finishing before starting new work
    • Bottlenecks become visible and can be addressed immediately
    • Improves quality through focus and reduced multitasking

    🌊 What Is "Flow"?

    Flow is the movement of customer value through a system. In Kanban, the goal is to achieve smooth, predictable, continuous flow — eliminating waste, idle time, and blockers that slow work down.

    • Ideal flow: Work moves steadily from request to delivery without waiting in queues
    • Disrupted flow: Work piles up between stages; teams are overloaded; delivery is unpredictable
    • Flow metrics: Cycle time, lead time, throughput, and WIP measure flow health objectively

    💡 Kanban Key Concepts

    🗂️ Work Item

    A unit of work flowing through the system (ticket, story, task). Represented by a card on the Kanban board.

    🚧 WIP Limit

    Work-In-Progress limit — a cap on how many items can be in a workflow stage simultaneously. Forces focus and exposes bottlenecks.

    🔄 Workflow Stage

    A step in the delivery process (e.g., Analysis, Development, Testing). Columns on the Kanban board represent stages.

    ⏱️ Lead Time

    Total time from customer request (commitment) to delivery. Measures the customer's experience of wait time end-to-end.

    🔁 Cycle Time

    Time from when work starts (active) to done. Measures the team's processing speed for items they've begun.

    📈 Throughput

    Number of work items completed per unit of time (e.g., 5 items/week). Key measure of team capacity for forecasting.

    🔢 Core Principles & Practices

    principles practices cadences
    The Kanban Method is built on two sets of foundational principles and six core practices. Together they provide a philosophical foundation for evolutionary change and practical guidance for improving any service delivery system.

    🏛️ Foundational Principles

    📋 Change Management Principles

    1. Start with what you do now

    Kanban does not prescribe a specific process or role structure. It respects existing processes, roles, and responsibilities — and uses them as the starting point for evolutionary improvement.

    2. Agree to pursue incremental, evolutionary change

    Small, evolutionary changes rather than large disruptions. This reduces resistance and risk while building momentum through early wins and continuous improvement.

    3. Initially, respect current processes, roles and job titles

    Unlike frameworks that require role changes, Kanban preserves existing structures — reducing fear and resistance to adoption, especially at the start.

    4. Encourage acts of leadership at all levels

    Leadership and improvement are everyone's responsibility. Kanban empowers individuals at all levels to identify and drive improvements, not just managers.

    🎯 Service Delivery Principles

    1. Understand and focus on customer needs and expectations

    The purpose of any service is to meet customer needs. Start with understanding who your customers are, what they need, and what quality of service they expect.

    2. Manage the work; let people self-organize around it

    Work items are managed (prioritized, limited, tracked) and the team self-organizes around pulling them. This fosters autonomy and ownership within clear boundaries.

    3. Regularly review the network of services and their policies

    The overall system and its policies are regularly inspected and adapted to meet changing business needs and customer expectations.

    ⚙️ The 6 Core Practices

    The six practices define what a Kanban implementation looks like in action. They build on each other — you cannot skip to practice 6 without first implementing practice 1.

    1
    👁️ Visualize the Workflow

    Map all work to a board. Make the work items, workflow steps, policies, and blockers visible to everyone. What you can see, you can improve.

    Examples: Kanban board, columns per workflow stage, color-coded card types, blocked item indicators

    2
    🚧 Limit Work-In-Progress (WIP)

    Cap the number of items in any stage simultaneously. This forces focus, reduces multitasking, and exposes bottlenecks that would otherwise stay hidden.

    Rule: "Stop starting, start finishing!" Items should flow through, not accumulate.

    3
    🌊 Manage Flow

    Monitor and optimize the movement of work items through the system. Track flow metrics (cycle time, throughput, CFD) to identify and eliminate bottlenecks and delays.

    Goal: Smooth, predictable flow — minimal waiting, maximum throughput.

    4
    📋 Make Policies Explicit

    Define and document the rules of the system: entry/exit criteria, Definition of Done, WIP limits, escalation policies. Explicit policies reduce ambiguity and enable objective discussions.

    Examples: "Ready" criteria, column definitions, how blocked items are handled

    5
    🔁 Implement Feedback Loops

    Establish regular cadences for reviewing work, processes, and results. Kanban defines 7 feedback cadences ranging from daily standups to quarterly strategy reviews, ensuring continuous learning.

    Daily Standup, Replenishment, Delivery Planning, Service Delivery Review, Risk Review, Operations Review, Strategy Review

    6
    📈 Improve Collaboratively, Evolve Experimentally

    Use models and data to guide collaborative improvement. Teams form shared theories, run experiments to test them, and use results to evolve policies and workflows — the scientific method applied to process improvement.

    Approach: Theory of Constraints, Systems Thinking, kaizen mindset

    📅 The 7 Kanban Cadences

    🔁 Feedback Loops at Every Timescale

    Kanban uses seven recurring meetings (cadences) to build feedback loops from daily to quarterly:

    Cadence Frequency Purpose
    Daily Kanban Meeting Daily Coordinate flow, surface blockers, update board status
    Replenishment Meeting Weekly Pull new work into the system based on available capacity
    Delivery Planning Weekly / Bi-weekly Forecast delivery dates using flow data and Monte Carlo
    Service Delivery Review Monthly Review fitness-for-purpose; measure customer satisfaction
    Risk Review Monthly Identify, classify, and mitigate delivery risks
    Operations Review Monthly Balance demand and capability across the organization
    Strategy Review Quarterly Evaluate fitness of services against market and business strategy

    🏊 Swim Lanes

    Swim lanes are horizontal rows on a Kanban board that separate different categories of work, teams, or service classes. They allow teams to manage different types of work with different policies side-by-side on the same board.

    🚨 Expedite Lane

    Critical/emergency work (production outages). Bypasses normal WIP limits. Should be used sparingly — if used too often, it indicates a systemic problem.

    📅 Fixed Date Lane

    Work with hard deadlines (regulatory, conference demo). Cost of delay spikes at the deadline. Needs special scheduling attention.

    📋 Standard Lane

    Regular work flowing at normal pace. Managed by standard WIP limits and FIFO prioritization. Most items belong here.

    💡 Intangible Lane

    Technical debt, refactoring, or research items. No immediate deadline but real future business value. Fill during idle capacity.

    📊 Classes of Service

    Class Examples Cost of Delay Priority Rule
    🚨 Expedite Production outage, critical security patch Very high — growing rapidly every hour Drop everything; limit to 1 at a time
    📅 Fixed Date Regulatory deadline, conference demo Negligible, then sudden spike at deadline Schedule carefully; monitor progress
    📋 Standard Normal features and stories Grows linearly over time FIFO within class
    💡 Intangible Tech debt, documentation, research Low immediate; high long-term Fill idle capacity

    📊 Kanban Metrics & Flow

    metrics flow forecasting
    Kanban is a data-driven method. Flow metrics provide an objective view of system health and predictability. Unlike velocity (Scrum-specific), Kanban metrics are based on actual historical data — no estimation required — making forecasts more reliable over time.

    ⏱️ The Four Flow Metrics

    ⏱️ Lead Time

    Total time from request to delivery

    Measured from when an item is committed (enters the backlog) to when it's delivered. This is what the customer experiences as wait time.

    Lead Time = Done Date − Commitment Date

    🔁 Cycle Time

    Time from work start to completion

    Measured from when work actively begins ("In Progress") to "Done". This is what the team experiences — their actual processing time per item.

    Cycle Time = Done Date − Start Date

    📈 Throughput

    Items completed per unit of time

    Count of work items finished in a time period (e.g., 8 items/week). The most reliable metric for forecasting — more stable than velocity in story points.

    Throughput = Items Done ÷ Time Period

    🚧 WIP (Work In Progress)

    Items actively being worked on right now

    The number of items in the system at any given moment (excluding backlog and done). High WIP indicates overload. Governed by Little's Law.

    Cycle Time = WIP ÷ Throughput

    ⚖️ Little's Law

    The Fundamental Equation of Flow

    Cycle Time = WIP ÷ Throughput
    Cycle Time
    Time per item
    WIP
    Items in system
    Throughput
    Items/time
    Practical implication: If WIP increases and throughput stays the same, cycle time must increase. To speed up delivery, either increase throughput or — more practically — reduce WIP. Reducing WIP is almost always faster and easier.

    📉 Flow Efficiency

    Flow Efficiency = (Active Time ÷ Total Lead Time) × 100%

    Measures the percentage of time work is actively being worked on vs. waiting in queues. Most teams are shocked to discover their flow efficiency is only 5–15%.

    ❌ Typical: 5–15%
    85–95% of the time, work is just waiting in queues between stages
    ✅ World-class: 40%+
    High-performing teams minimize handoffs and queue time dramatically

    📊 Cumulative Flow Diagram (CFD)

    📈 Reading the CFD

    A stacked area chart showing the count of work items in each workflow stage over time. The most powerful visual for understanding system health:

    • Widening band in a stage → work is accumulating there (bottleneck detected)
    • Parallel bands of consistent width → smooth, predictable flow
    • Flat top "Done" line → work entering the system but not getting completed
    • Vertical distance between two band boundaries → WIP in that stage at that moment
    • Horizontal distance between two band boundaries → approximate cycle time for that stage

    🎲 Monte Carlo Forecasting

    🔮 Probabilistic Forecasting — No Estimation Required

    Monte Carlo simulation uses historical throughput data to run thousands of simulations and answer questions like:

    • "When will these 20 items be done?" → probability distribution of dates (e.g., 70% chance by May 15, 90% chance by May 22)
    • "How many items can we deliver in 4 weeks?" → a range with confidence levels, not a single point estimate

    Key advantage: No story pointing or estimation sessions needed — just count completed items per week. Accuracy improves as historical data grows.

    🎯 Metrics Anti-Patterns

    🚫 Avoid These Mistakes

    • Using throughput to compare different teams — each team's context is unique
    • Ignoring outliers in cycle time data — they reveal systemic problems
    • Optimizing one metric at the expense of others (throughput at the cost of quality)
    • Setting throughput targets that incentivize cutting corners or gaming the system
    • Measuring activity (cards moved) instead of outcomes (value delivered to customers)
    • Abandoning metrics after one bad week — look at trends over 6–8 weeks minimum

    ⚖️ Kanban vs Scrum

    Both make work visible to the whole team and stakeholders through boards and metrics

    🔄 Inspect & Adapt

    Both emphasize continuous improvement based on empirical feedback and data

    👥 Team Focus

    Both prioritize team collaboration, self-organization, and collective ownership of work

    🚀 Customer Value

    Both focus on delivering value to customers efficiently and continuously

    🏃 Agile & Lean Roots

    Both are grounded in agile and lean thinking — reduce waste, maximize value

    📊 Data-Driven

    Both use metrics to make informed decisions, improve forecasting, and drive improvements

    🎯 When to Use Each

    🎴 Choose Kanban When…

    • Work arrives continuously and unpredictably
    • Team handles operational or support work
    • Items vary greatly in size and urgency
    • Team needs minimal process overhead
    • Continuous deployment is already in place
    • Team is highly mature and self-directed
    • Working in a maintenance or help-desk context

    🎯 Choose Scrum When…

    • Building a new product or significant feature set
    • Team needs regular planning and review cadences
    • Stakeholders want regular deliverables and reviews
    • Team is newer to agile and benefits from structure
    • Work can be planned in short, time-boxed iterations
    • Product vision evolves through regular stakeholder feedback
    • Cross-functional collaboration is a core requirement

    🔀 Scrumban

    scrumban hybrid PSK
    Scrumban combines the structured cadences and roles of Scrum with the flow management and WIP limits of Kanban. It gives teams the best of both worlds — direction and rhythm from Scrum, flow efficiency and data-driven forecasting from Kanban.

    📜 Origins of Scrumban

    Corey Ladas coined "Scrumban" in his 2008 book, describing it as a transition path from Scrum toward Kanban. It has since evolved into its own hybrid approach. Today, Professional Scrum with Kanban (PSK I) from Scrum.org formally defines how Kanban practices can enhance Scrum without replacing or diluting it.

    🏗️ What Scrumban Keeps, Drops, and Adds

    🎯 Retains from Scrum
    • Scrum roles (PO, Scrum Master, Developers)
    • Sprint cadence (time-boxed iterations)
    • Sprint Review & Retrospective
    • Product Backlog & Sprint Backlog
    • Sprint Goal commitment
    🎴 Adds from Kanban
    • Explicit WIP limits on the Sprint board
    • Detailed workflow visualization (sub-columns)
    • Flow metrics — cycle time and throughput
    • Classes of service for urgency management
    • Continuous improvement mindset (kaizen)
    🔧 Key Changes in Practice
    • Sprint board has WIP limits per column
    • Work is pulled, not assigned by PO
    • Metrics focus on flow, not just velocity
    • Backlog replenished on demand (kanban trigger)
    • More flexibility to accommodate urgent work mid-Sprint

    🎓 Professional Scrum with Kanban (PSK)

    PSK I — Scrum.org Certification

    The PSK curriculum from Scrum.org defines four focus areas for applying Kanban within Scrum to improve flow without abandoning the Scrum framework:

    1
    Visualize the Work

    Make the Sprint workflow visible with a multi-column board showing item states from backlog to done. Expose blockers and bottlenecks so the team can act on them daily.

    2
    Limit WIP

    Apply WIP limits to Sprint board columns to prevent overloading. This enforces "stop starting, start finishing" and surfaces bottlenecks within the Sprint itself.

    3
    Manage Flow

    Track cycle time and throughput during each Sprint. Use this data to improve Sprint Planning forecasts and make Retrospective improvement conversations more targeted.

    4
    Improve Continuously

    Use Sprint Retrospectives combined with flow data to drive targeted improvements. Focus on reducing cycle time, improving throughput, and eliminating waste in the delivery pipeline.

    Teams with strong Scrum fundamentals ready to optimize further. Uses Scrum as scaffolding while adopting lean flow principles.

    ⚠️ Scrumban Anti-Patterns

    • "No Sprint, no roles" Scrumban: Just using a Kanban board and calling it Scrumban — this is just Kanban, not Scrumban
    • Abandoning retrospectives: Without structured improvement loops, neither Scrum nor Kanban functions well long-term
    • WIP limits as suggestions: WIP limits only deliver value if the team enforces them consistently — every day
    • Ignoring flow metrics: Using a Kanban board visually but not measuring cycle time or throughput misses most of the benefit
    • Sprint commitment + Kanban pull mixed: Pick one commitment model and stick to it — mixing them creates confusion and accountability gaps

    📐 The 10 SAFe Principles

    1. Take an Economic View

    Deliver the best value and quality in the shortest sustainable lead time. Understand the economic impact of every decision using Cost of Delay.

    2. Apply Systems Thinking

    Understand the system — the solution, the enterprise, and the value stream — to optimize the whole, not just parts.

    3. Assume Variability; Preserve Options

    Maintain multiple requirements and design options longer. Make decisions at the Last Responsible Moment using Set-Based Design.

    4. Build Incrementally with Fast Integrated Learning Cycles

    Integration points create knowledge. Short iterations with frequent integration reduce risk and accelerate learning.

    5. Base Milestones on Objective Evaluation of Working Systems

    Evaluate progress on actual working software at integration points, not on completion of documents or phases.

    6. Make Value Flow Without Interruptions

    Identify and eliminate impediments to flow. Visualize work, limit WIP, reduce batch sizes, and manage queue lengths.

    7. Apply Cadence, Synchronize with Cross-Domain Planning

    Cadence makes uncertainty manageable. Synchronization across teams and ARTs enables effective multi-team coordination.

    8. Unlock the Intrinsic Motivation of Knowledge Workers

    Create an environment of autonomy, mastery, and purpose. Management's role is to create conditions for peak performance, not to direct activity.

    9. Decentralize Decision-Making

    Reserve strategic decisions for leadership; delegate tactical decisions to teams. Speed requires decisions made by those with context.

    10. Organize Around Value

    Design the organization to deliver value efficiently. Value Streams align people, processes, and technology to customer value — not functional silos.

    🚂 Agile Release Train (ART)

    ART PI Planning program increment
    The Agile Release Train (ART) is the primary value delivery construct in SAFe. A long-lived team of Agile teams (50–125 people) aligned to a common mission, operating on a shared cadence called the Program Increment (PI).

    🚂 What Is an ART?

    Key Facts

    • Size: 50–125 people (5–12 Agile teams)
    • Cadence: 8–12 week Planning Intervals
    • Mission: Aligned to a single value stream
    • Output: Integrated, potentially shippable increment every PI
    • Long-lived: Teams stay together; the train doesn't stop

    ART Metaphor

    Like a train with a schedule, the ART runs on a fixed cadence. Teams board the train (PI Planning), deliver features throughout the PI, and demonstrate integrated value at the end. The train keeps running — there is no stop between PIs.

    📐 Level-by-Level Detail

    🏢

    Epic — Portfolio Level

    Timeline: 3 months to 1+ year · Spans multiple PIs
    What it is: The largest unit of work in SAFe — a strategic initiative that is too large to complete in a single PI. Epics can be Business Epics (customer-facing) or Enabler Epics (architectural / infrastructure).
    Entry criteria: Lightweight Business Case (LBC) approved, benefit hypothesis defined, MVP scoped, WSJF score calculated, and Epic placed on the Portfolio Kanban.
    Exit / split trigger: Epic moves to "Implementing" on Portfolio Kanban → Epic Owner collaborates with Product Management to split into Features (or Capabilities in Large Solution).
    Key metrics: Business value delivered vs hypothesis, flow time on Portfolio Kanban, Return on Investment vs forecast.
    ⚙️

    Capability — Solution Level (optional — Large Solution SAFe only)

    Timeline: 1–2 PIs · 12–24 weeks
    What it is: A higher-level solution behaviour or function. Similar in structure to a Feature but scoped to the Solution Train level — too large for a single ART in one PI.
    When to use: Only in Large Solution SAFe when multiple ARTs collaborate on a single solution. Managed on the Solution Backlog by Solution Management.
    Split into: 3–8 Features, each sized for one ART to deliver within one PI. Each Feature must be independently valuable and testable.
    Key events: Pre-PI Planning (Solution Train); Solution Demo at PI end; Solution Train Sync during the PI.
    📦

    Feature — Program Level

    Timeline: 1 PI · 8–12 weeks · fits within one ART increment
    What it is: A service or capability that delivers business value to the customer. Written as: "[Action] so that [benefit]". Includes a benefit hypothesis and acceptance criteria.
    Ready criteria: WSJF prioritised, benefit hypothesis written, acceptance criteria defined, no unresolved dependencies. Sized to fit within one PI (~10 team iterations combined).
    Split into: 4–10 User Stories distributed across the PI Iterations. Use workflow steps, data types, or rule variations to split. Each story must implement part of the feature's acceptance criteria.
    Key events: Selected at PI Planning · progress reviewed at System Demo each Iteration · completed by end of PI.
    📝

    User Story — Team Level

    Timeline: 1–2 Iterations · 1–8 story points
    Format: "As a [persona], I want [goal], so that [benefit]." Acceptance criteria written as Given/When/Then (BDD style). Must meet the team's Definition of Ready before Sprint Planning.
    INVEST criteria: Independent · Negotiable · Valuable · Estimable · Small · Testable. Any story failing multiple criteria should be re-written or split.
    Sizing: 1–8 story points (Fibonacci). Stories > 8 points must be split. Use relative estimation (Planning Poker, T-shirt sizing) to size during Backlog Refinement.
    Split patterns: Workflow steps · Data variations · Business rules · Happy path + edge cases · CRUD operations · Defer performance / security as Enabler Stories.
    ✅

    Task — Iteration Level

    Timeline: 1–2 days · max 8–16 hours of work
    What it is: The lowest unit of planned work. Created by the Development Team during Sprint Planning by decomposing accepted Stories. Tasks are tracked on the Iteration Board (physical or digital Kanban board).
    Characteristics: Concrete and actionable (e.g., "Write unit tests for login API"). No estimation — tracked as hours remaining or simple status (To Do · In Progress · Done).
    Self-assignment: Team members pull tasks based on skill, capacity, and priority during Daily Scrum. No individual is assigned a task by a manager — pull is key to flow.
    Built-in Quality: Tasks should include testing, code review, and documentation work — not just coding. "Done" means meets the team's Definition of Done (DoD).

    💼 Lean Portfolio Management

    LPM WSJF value streams lean budgets
    Lean Portfolio Management (LPM) aligns strategy with execution by connecting the enterprise's strategic goals to the ARTs and value streams that deliver them. It replaces traditional project-based funding with Lean Budgets tied to value streams.

    🔁 Value Streams

    A Value Stream is the sequence of steps an organization uses to build solutions that deliver a continuous flow of value to a customer.

    Operational Value Streams

    Steps to deliver a product or service to an end customer (e.g., order-to-cash). These are the processes the business runs.

    Development Value Streams

    Steps to build and support systems used by operational value streams. Funded via Lean Budgets; organized into ARTs.

    ⚖️ WSJF — Weighted Shortest Job First

    What Is WSJF?

    WSJF is SAFe's prioritization model for sequencing work to maximize economic benefit. Features are ranked by the ratio of their Cost of Delay to Job Duration:

    WSJF = Cost of Delay ÷ Job Duration (Size)
    ComponentWhat It Measures
    User/Business ValueHow much do users or the business benefit?
    Time CriticalityHow does value decay with time? (regulatory deadlines, market windows)
    Risk Reduction / Opportunity EnablementDoes this reduce risk or unlock future value?
    Job SizeHow long will this take? (Story points, T-shirt size)

    Rule: Do the highest WSJF items first — small, high-CoD items get top priority over large, low-CoD items.

    ⚖️ WSJF Prioritization — How It Works

    flowchart TD BV["Business Value\nHow much do users and the business benefit?"] TC["Time Criticality\nHow does value decay over time?\nDeadlines · Market windows"] RR["Risk Reduction\nOpportunity Enablement\nDoes this reduce risk or unlock future value?"] COD["Cost of Delay\nBV plus TC plus RR\nCombined urgency and value"] SIZE["Job Size\nEstimated duration or effort\nStory points · T-shirt size"] WSJF["WSJF Score\nCost of Delay divided by Job Size\nHighest score = Do FIRST"] BV --> COD TC --> COD RR --> COD COD --> WSJF SIZE --> WSJF

    💰 Lean Budgets & Guardrails

    From Project Funding to Value Stream Funding

    Traditional project funding approves individual projects and disbands teams when done. SAFe's Lean Budgets fund value streams continuously, keeping stable teams together:

    • No project-by-project approval: Value streams have an annual budget with quarterly adjustments
    • Guardrails: Lightweight governance rules (e.g., spending caps, CapEx/OpEx ratio) that replace detailed project approval processes
    • Participatory Budgeting: ARTs and Business Owners collaboratively set spending allocations during PI Planning
    • Dynamic reallocation: Budgets can shift between value streams based on strategic needs each PI

    📊 Portfolio Kanban

    The Portfolio Kanban system manages the flow of portfolio epics from ideation through implementation:

    Funnel → Reviewing → Analyzing → Portfolio Backlog → Implementing → Done
    • Lean Business Case: Epics require an LBC before proceeding to Analyzing
    • WIP limits at each stage prevent overloading the portfolio
    • Epic Owners shepherd epics through the kanban and coordinate implementation across ARTs

    🎯 Portfolio Epics

    Business Epics

    Deliver direct business value. Customer-facing features that span multiple PIs and require cross-ART coordination.

    Enabler Epics

    Build the architectural runway and technical infrastructure that future business epics will depend on. Not directly customer-visible but strategically critical.

    📖 Glossary

    terms definitions
    A comprehensive glossary of Agile, Scrum, Kanban, and SAFe terminology to help you understand the key concepts, roles, artifacts, events, and practices across the Scrum framework, the Kanban Method, and the Scaled Agile Framework.

    A

    🧪 A/B Testing
    An experimentation method used in Product Discovery where two versions of a product or feature are compared to determine which performs better. Helps teams make data-driven decisions about product changes.
    ✅ Acceptance Criteria
    Conditions that a software product must satisfy to be accepted by a user, customer, or other stakeholder. Defines when a user story is complete and working as expected.
    🔄 Adapt
    One of the three pillars of Scrum (along with Transparency and Inspection). If an inspector determines that one or more aspects of a process deviate outside acceptable limits, adjustments must be made as soon as possible.
    🎯 Agile
    An iterative approach to project management and software development that helps teams deliver value faster with fewer headaches. Based on the Agile Manifesto and 12 principles.
    📜 Agile Manifesto
    A declaration of four key values and 12 principles to guide an iterative and people-centric approach to software development. Created in 2001 by 17 software developers.
    🚂 Agile Release Train (ART)
    (SAFe) The primary value-delivery construct in SAFe. A long-lived, cross-functional team of Agile teams (50–125 people) aligned to a common mission and operating on a shared cadence called the Program Increment (PI). An ART plans, commits, builds, and delivers together. Governed by the Release Train Engineer (RTE).
    🏗️ Architectural Runway
    (SAFe) The existing code, components, and technical infrastructure that supports near-term delivery of business features without excessive re-design or delay. Maintained through Enabler work in each PI. Without sufficient runway, every business feature drags along hidden technical debt.

    B

    📚 Backlog
    A list of work items (user stories, features, bugs) that need to be completed. Can refer to Product Backlog or Sprint Backlog.
    📋 Backlog Grooming (Refinement)
    The ongoing process of reviewing, prioritizing, and refining Product Backlog items to ensure they are ready for upcoming Sprints. Includes adding details, estimates, and ordering items.
    🧪 Behavior-Driven Development (BDD)
    An Agile development practice that extends Test-Driven Development by using natural language to describe test cases. Focuses on behavior rather than implementation. Uses Given-When-Then format for scenarios.
    📉 Burndown Chart
    A graphical representation of work left to do versus time. Shows remaining work in the Sprint (Sprint Burndown) or release (Release Burndown). Helps track progress toward Sprint Goal.
    🔥 Burnout
    A state of physical, emotional, and mental exhaustion caused by prolonged stress and overwork. Modern Scrum teams prioritize sustainable pace, psychological safety, and work-life balance to prevent burnout.
    📈 Burnup Chart
    A graphical representation showing completed work versus time. Opposite of a burndown chart, it tracks work completed rather than work remaining.
    🏆 Business Agility
    (SAFe) The ability of an enterprise to compete and thrive in the digital age by quickly adapting to market and environmental changes in productive and cost-effective ways. SAFe's ultimate goal. Achieved through mastery of the 7 Core Competencies: Team & Technical Agility, Agile Product Delivery, Enterprise Solution Delivery, Lean Portfolio Management, Organisational Agility, Continuous Learning Culture, and Lean-Agile Leadership.

    C

    ✔ Code Review
    The systematic examination of source code by team members to find defects, improve code quality, and share knowledge. Often part of the Definition of Done in modern Scrum teams.
    🔄 Continuous Delivery (CD)
    A software development practice where code changes are automatically prepared for release to production. Every change that passes automated tests is ready to be deployed but requires manual approval.
    🚀 Continuous Deployment
    An extension of Continuous Delivery where every change that passes automated tests is automatically deployed to production without manual intervention. Enables the fastest possible time-to-market.
    🔄 Continuous Improvement (Kaizen)
    An ongoing effort to improve products, services, or processes through incremental improvements over time. Core principle in Agile and Scrum.
    🔧 Continuous Integration (CI)
    A development practice where developers integrate code into a shared repository frequently (multiple times per day). Each integration is verified by automated build and tests to detect integration errors quickly.
    👥 Cross-Functional Team
    A team with all the competencies needed to accomplish the work without depending on others outside the team. The Development Team in Scrum is cross-functional.
    ⏱️ Cycle Time
    The time from when work begins on an item until it's completed and delivered. A key metric for team efficiency. Shorter cycle times indicate faster delivery and better flow.
    🗂️ Class of Service
    A policy-defined category in Kanban that dictates how a work item should be handled based on its cost of delay and urgency. Common classes: Expedite (critical, bypasses WIP limits), Fixed Date (hard deadline), Standard (normal linear cost of delay), and Intangible (tech debt / research, low immediate urgency). Determines prioritization rules and swim lane assignment on the board.
    📊 Cumulative Flow Diagram (CFD)
    A stacked area chart in Kanban showing the count of work items in each workflow stage over time. Reveals bottlenecks (widening bands in a stage), smooth flow (parallel constant-width bands), and provides a visual view of WIP, approximate cycle time, and throughput at any point in time. One of the most powerful flow visualization tools.

    D

    🔄 Daily Scrum (Daily Standup)
    A 15-minute time-boxed event for the Development Team to synchronize activities and create a plan for the next 24 hours. Held at the same time and place each day. Team members answer three questions: What did I do yesterday? What will I do today? What's blocking me?
    ✅ Definition of Done (DoD)
    A shared understanding of what it means for work to be complete. When a Product Backlog item meets the Definition of Done, an Increment is born. Ensures transparency and quality.
    ✅ Definition of Ready
    Criteria that a user story must meet before being accepted into a Sprint during Sprint Planning. Ensures backlog items are clear, feasible, and testable.
    🔧 DevOps
    A set of practices that combines software development (Dev) and IT operations (Ops) to shorten systems development lifecycle and provide continuous delivery with high quality. Emphasizes automation, collaboration, and monitoring.
    🎨 Discovery (Product Discovery)
    The process of exploring problems, understanding user needs, and validating ideas before committing to building solutions. Uses techniques like user research, prototyping, and experimentation. Often runs in parallel with delivery in Dual-Track Agile.
    🛤️ Dual-Track Agile
    An approach where Discovery and Delivery run in parallel tracks. Discovery explores problems and validates ideas, while Delivery builds and ships validated solutions. Enables continuous learning and reduces waste.
    👥 Development Team
    Professionals who do the work of delivering a potentially releasable Increment of "Done" product at the end of each Sprint. Self-organizing and cross-functional. Optimal size is 3-9 members.

    E

    🧠 Empiricism
    The theory that knowledge comes from experience and making decisions based on what is known. Scrum is founded on empiricism. It asserts that knowledge comes from experience and making decisions based on what is observed.
    ⛰ Epic
    A large user story that cannot be delivered as defined within a single Sprint. Typically broken down into smaller user stories that can fit within a Sprint.
    📊 Evidence-Based Management (EBM)
    An empirical approach to help organizations continuously improve customer outcomes, organizational capabilities, and business results. Uses Key Value Areas: Current Value, Unrealized Value, Time-to-Market, and Ability to Innovate.
    🔧 Enabler
    (SAFe) Work that builds the Architectural Runway and technical infrastructure needed to support future business features. Enablers exist at every level: Enabler Stories (team), Enabler Features (ART), and Enabler Epics (Portfolio). They are prioritised alongside business work and funded through Lean Budgets.
    🎫 Epic Owner
    (SAFe) The person responsible for shepherding a Portfolio Epic through the Portfolio Kanban, coordinating its implementation across ARTs and Value Streams. Creates the Lean Business Case, defines the Minimum Viable Product (MVP), tracks progress, and communicates status to stakeholders.

    F

    ✨ Feature
    A service or functionality that fulfills a stakeholder need. Features are typically larger than user stories and may span multiple Sprints. Often decomposed into smaller stories for implementation.
    🔢 Fibonacci Sequence
    A numerical sequence (1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13, 21...) commonly used in Agile estimation. Reflects the uncertainty and increasing complexity of larger work items. Used in Planning Poker for story point estimation.
    🔄 Flow
    The movement of work items through the development process. Kanban and Lean practices focus on optimizing flow by reducing waste, bottlenecks, and work-in-progress limits.
    ⚡ Flow Efficiency
    A Kanban metric measuring the percentage of time work is actively being worked on versus waiting in queues. Calculated as: (Active Time ÷ Total Lead Time) × 100%. Most teams discover their flow efficiency is only 5–15%, meaning work spends 85–95% of its time waiting. High-performing teams achieve 40%+. Improving flow efficiency is often the fastest way to reduce lead time.

    G

    🎯 Goal (Product Goal, Sprint Goal)
    An objective that provides purpose and direction. Product Goal is the long-term objective for the product. Sprint Goal is the single objective for the Sprint that provides focus and coherence.
    📈 Grooming (Refinement)
    See "Backlog Refinement". The term "Grooming" has been replaced by "Refinement" in the Scrum Guide 2020. The ongoing process of reviewing and refining Product Backlog items.

    H

    💡 How Might We (HMW)
    A Design Thinking technique used in Product Discovery to frame problem statements as opportunities. Transforms challenges into actionable questions. Example: "How might we improve user onboarding?"
    🏡 Hybrid Work
    A work model that combines remote and in-office work. Scrum teams using hybrid models must adapt ceremonies and collaboration practices to ensure equal participation and psychological safety for all team members.

    I

    🚀 Increment
    The sum of all Product Backlog items completed during a Sprint and the value of the increments of all previous Sprints. Must be in useable condition and meet the Definition of Done. Potentially releasable.
    🔍 Inspect
    One of the three pillars of Scrum. Scrum users must frequently inspect Scrum artifacts and progress toward a Sprint Goal to detect undesirable variances.
    🔄 Iteration
    A time-boxed period during which work is completed. In Scrum, a Sprint is an iteration. Iterations enable teams to deliver working software frequently.
    🔬 Inspect & Adapt (I&A)
    (SAFe) A quarterly ART-level event held during the IP Iteration at the end of each PI. Consists of three parts: (1) PI System Demo — the integrated system is shown to stakeholders, (2) Quantitative & Qualitative Measurement — the ART reviews PI metrics and team health, and (3) Problem-Solving Workshop — root-cause analysis leading to improvement stories in the next PI. The ART's equivalent of a large-scale Retrospective.
    🛠️ IP Iteration (Innovation & Planning Iteration)
    (SAFe) The last iteration in a PI, reserved for three purposes: (1) hosting the Inspect & Adapt event and PI System Demo, (2) innovation — teams work on Spikes, hackathons, or tech debt, and (3) planning preparation for the next PI. Acts as a buffer preventing unfinished work from bleeding into the next PI and ensures teams have genuine innovation time each quarter.

    K

    🎴 Kanban
    A Lean method to manage and improve work across human systems. Emphasizes visualization of work, limiting work-in-progress (WIP), and optimizing flow. Can be combined with Scrum in "Scrumban" or "Professional Scrum with Kanban". Note: lowercase kanban = the visual signal card; capital-K Kanban = the complete management method.
    🗃️ Kanban Board
    A visual tool displaying work items as cards in columns representing workflow stages. Provides at-a-glance visibility into all active work, WIP counts, bottlenecks, blocked items, and team capacity. Can be physical (sticky notes on a wall) or digital (Jira, Azure DevOps, Trello). The primary visualization and management tool of the Kanban Method.
    🔁 Kanban Cadences
    The seven feedback loops defined by the Kanban Method at different time horizons: Daily Kanban Meeting, Replenishment Meeting, Delivery Planning, Service Delivery Review, Risk Review, Operations Review, and Strategy Review. Together they ensure continuous learning and adaptation at every organizational level.
    📈 Key Value Areas (KVAs)
    The four measurement areas in Evidence-Based Management: Current Value (CV), Unrealized Value (UV), Time-to-Market (T2M), and Ability to Innovate (A2I). Used to empirically assess and improve organizational performance.

    L

    ⏱️ Lead Time
    The time from when work is requested (enters the backlog) until it's delivered to the customer. A key metric for measuring flow efficiency and responsiveness to customer needs.
    📉 Lean
    A methodology focused on maximizing customer value while minimizing waste. Core principles: eliminate waste, amplify learning, decide as late as possible, deliver as fast as possible, empower the team, build integrity in, see the whole.
    🔗 LeSS (Large-Scale Scrum)
    A scaling framework that applies Scrum principles to multiple teams working on the same product. Emphasizes simplicity with one Product Owner, one Product Backlog, and one potentially shippable product increment.
    ⚖️ Little's Law
    A fundamental theorem of flow: Cycle Time = WIP ÷ Throughput. States that in a stable system, average cycle time equals average WIP divided by average throughput. Proves that reducing WIP is the most reliable and immediate lever to reduce cycle time. Foundational to Kanban's emphasis on WIP limits.
    🏛️ LACE (Lean-Agile Center of Excellence)
    (SAFe) A small, dedicated team of change agents whose mission is to guide and sustain the SAFe transformation across the enterprise. Established in Step 4 of the SAFe Implementation Roadmap. Responsibilities include coaching leaders and ARTs, maintaining the implementation roadmap, measuring progress, and continuously improving SAFe adoption.
    💼 Lean Portfolio Management (LPM)
    (SAFe) The SAFe competency that aligns enterprise strategy with execution through three key functions: Strategy & Investment Funding (WSJF prioritisation, Lean Budgets), Agile Portfolio Operations (coordinating ARTs and Value Streams), and Lean Governance (spending guardrails, forecast & budget dynamics). Replaces traditional project-based portfolio management with value-stream funding.

    M

    📊 Metrics
    Quantifiable measures used to track and assess the performance of Agile teams and products. Common metrics: velocity, cycle time, lead time, burndown/burnup, sprint goal success rate, customer satisfaction.
    📦 Minimum Viable Product (MVP)
    The smallest version of a product that can be released to gather validated learning about customers. Contains just enough features to satisfy early customers and provide feedback for future development.
    👥 Mob Programming
    An extension of pair programming where the entire team works on the same thing, at the same time, in the same space, on the same computer. Promotes knowledge sharing, quality, and team collaboration.
    🎲 Monte Carlo Forecasting
    A probabilistic simulation technique used in Kanban that uses historical throughput data to answer questions like "when will these X items be done?" or "how many items can we complete by date Y?" Runs thousands of simulations to produce a probability distribution of outcomes (e.g., 70% chance by May 15, 90% chance by May 22) instead of a single-point estimate. Requires no story pointing — accuracy improves as historical data grows.

    N

    🔗 Nexus
    A scaling framework developed by Scrum.org for coordinating 3-9 Scrum Teams working on a single product. Introduces the Nexus Integration Team and additional artifacts and events to manage dependencies.
    ⚙️ Non-Functional Requirements (NFRs)
    Quality attributes and constraints that define how a system performs, such as performance, security, scalability, reliability, and usability. Should be incorporated into the Definition of Done.

    O

    🎯 Outcome
    The change in user behavior or business results that occurs from delivering features. Focuses on "why" we're building something. Contrasts with outputs (what we build). Modern Product Owners prioritize outcomes over outputs.
    📦 Output
    The features, products, or deliverables produced by a team. Represents "what" is being built. While important, outputs should be driven by desired outcomes rather than being goals themselves.

    P

    � Pair Programming
    An Agile software development technique where two programmers work together at one workstation. One writes code (driver) while the other reviews each line (navigator). Improves code quality and knowledge sharing.
    👤 Persona
    A fictional character created to represent a user type. Used in Product Discovery to help teams understand user needs, goals, behaviors, and pain points. Informs user story creation and product decisions.
    🔄 Pipeline (CI/CD Pipeline)
    An automated sequence of stages that code goes through from development to production. Includes building, testing, and deployment. Enables Continuous Integration and Continuous Delivery/Deployment.
    📚 Product Backlog
    An ordered list of everything that is known to be needed in the product. The single source of requirements for any changes to be made. Owned and prioritized by the Product Owner.
    📝 Product Backlog Item (PBI)
    An item in the Product Backlog. Can be a user story, feature, enhancement, bug fix, technical debt item, or knowledge acquisition task.
    🎯 Product Goal
    The long-term objective for the Scrum Team. Describes a future state of the product and serves as a target to plan against. The Product Backlog emerges to define what will fulfill the Product Goal.
    👑 Product Owner (PO)
    The person responsible for maximizing the value of the product and managing the Product Backlog. Single person (not a committee) who represents stakeholders and makes final decisions on product direction.
    🎨 Prototype
    An early sample or model of a product built to test a concept or process. Used in Product Discovery to validate ideas with users before full development. Can range from low-fidelity sketches to high-fidelity interactive mockups.
    🛡️ Psychological Safety
    A shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking. Team members feel comfortable expressing ideas, asking questions, admitting mistakes, and challenging each other without fear of punishment or humiliation. Critical for high-performing teams.
    🔽 Professional Scrum with Kanban (PSK)
    A Scrum.org curriculum and certification (PSK I) that teaches teams how to apply Kanban practices within Scrum to improve flow without abandoning the Scrum framework. Focuses on four areas: visualize work, limit WIP, manage flow, and improve continuously. Demonstrates that Kanban and Scrum are complementary, not competing.
    ⬅️ Pull System
    A work management approach where team members pull work items onto their board when they have capacity, rather than having work pushed onto them by managers or schedules. Core concept in Kanban. Prevents overloading, reduces WIP and context switching, ensures focus on finishing before starting new work. Contrasts with push systems where work is assigned regardless of capacity.
    📅 PI (Program Increment)
    (SAFe) A timebox of typically 8–12 weeks (4–6 two-week iterations plus one IP Iteration) during which an ART delivers incremental value in the form of working, tested software. The PI is SAFe's primary planning and delivery cadence — the ART equivalent of a Sprint. PIs make the progress of value delivery predictable and visible at scale.
    📋 PI Planning
    (SAFe) A two-day, face-to-face event for the entire ART (all teams simultaneously) that launches each PI. Teams create their team iteration plans, identify cross-team dependencies, set PI Objectives, vote ART confidence, and ROAM risks. Often considered the "heartbeat" of SAFe and the most critical event in the framework. Remote PI Planning is supported but in-person is strongly preferred.
    🎯 PI Objectives
    (SAFe) A summary of the business and technical goals each Agile team and the ART commit to achieving in the upcoming PI. Committed objectives are scored (1–10) by Business Owners during PI Planning. Stretch objectives are aspirational. Together they become the ART's "contract" for the PI and are used for ART Confidence Vote.
    🗂️ Portfolio Kanban
    (SAFe) A method used to visualise and manage the flow of Portfolio Epics from ideation through implementation. Kanban stages: Funnel → Reviewing → Analysing → Portfolio Backlog → Implementing → Done. Ensures only approved, prioritised Epics reach the ARTs and prevents uncontrolled scope entry at the portfolio level.
    📊 Program Board
    (SAFe) A large visual display created during PI Planning that shows all teams' planned features, cross-team dependencies (illustrated with strings/lines between sticky notes), and ART milestones for the upcoming PI. Makes the collective plan transparent and surfaces dependency risks immediately. An essential artefact of PI Planning.

    R

    � Refactoring
    The process of restructuring existing code without changing its external behavior. Improves code quality, maintainability, and reduces technical debt. A best practice in Agile development.
    ✨ Refinement (Backlog Refinement)
    The ongoing activity of adding detail, estimates, and order to Product Backlog items. Formerly called "Grooming". Not a formal Scrum event but an important continuous process.
    🚀 Release
    The transition of an increment from the development team to production where it can be used by customers. May occur at any time, potentially multiple times per Sprint.
    🏡 Remote Work
    A work arrangement where team members work from locations outside a central office. Requires adaptations to Scrum ceremonies, collaboration tools, and team practices to maintain effectiveness and team cohesion.
    🔄 Retrospective Action Items
    Specific improvements identified during Sprint Retrospectives that teams commit to implementing in the next Sprint. Should be tracked and reviewed to ensure continuous improvement happens.
    📊 RICE Scoring
    A prioritization framework (Reach × Impact × Confidence ÷ Effort) used by Product Owners to objectively prioritize features and initiatives. Helps make data-driven decisions about backlog ordering.
    📍 Roadmap
    A plan of action for how a product or solution will evolve over time. Provides a strategic context for the Product Backlog and Sprint planning.
    🗺️ ROAM Risks
    (SAFe) A risk classification technique used during PI Planning. All identified risks are placed on the Program Board and categorised as: Resolved (eliminated — no longer a risk), Owned (a specific person takes responsibility to manage it), Accepted (acknowledged with no mitigation action), or Mitigated (an action plan is defined to reduce probability or impact). Ensures ART-wide risk transparency.
    🚂 RTE (Release Train Engineer)
    (SAFe) The servant-leader and facilitator for the Agile Release Train. Described as the "Chief Scrum Master" for the ART. Responsibilities: facilitates PI Planning and all ART events, coaches Lean-Agile practices across teams, escalates impediments, manages risks, tracks ART metrics, and drives continuous improvement. One of the most impactful roles in a SAFe transformation.

    S

    🎯 Scrum
    A lightweight framework for developing, delivering, and sustaining complex products. Consists of Scrum Teams and their associated roles, events, artifacts, and rules. Founded on empiricism and lean thinking.
    👥 Scrum Master (SM)
    A servant-leader for the Scrum Team. Helps everyone understand Scrum theory, practices, rules, and values. Removes impediments, facilitates events, and coaches the team and organization.
    👥 Scrum Team
    Consists of a Product Owner, Development Team, and Scrum Master. Self-organizing and cross-functional. Designed to optimize flexibility, creativity, and productivity.
    🤝 Self-Organizing Team
    A team that chooses how best to accomplish their work, rather than being directed by others outside the team. Development Teams in Scrum are self-organizing.
    ⏱️ Sprint
    A time-box of one month or less during which a "Done", useable, and potentially releasable product Increment is created. New Sprint starts immediately after the previous Sprint ends. Contains all Scrum events.
    📋 Sprint Backlog
    The set of Product Backlog items selected for the Sprint, plus a plan for delivering the product Increment and realizing the Sprint Goal. Owned by the Development Team.
    ⛳ Sprint Goal
    An objective set for the Sprint that provides guidance on why the Increment is being built. Gives the Development Team flexibility regarding functionality implemented within the Sprint.
    📋 Sprint Planning
    A time-boxed event (4-8 hours) at the beginning of a Sprint where the Scrum Team plans the work to be performed. Creates the Sprint Goal and Sprint Backlog. Answers: What can be delivered? How will the work be done?
    🔄 Sprint Retrospective
    A time-boxed event (2-4 hours) after Sprint Review where the Scrum Team inspects itself and creates a plan for improvements. Focuses on people, relationships, process, and tools. Answers: What went well? What could be improved? How can we improve?
    ✅ Sprint Review
    A time-boxed event (1.5-4 hours) at the end of Sprint where the team inspects the Increment and adapts the Product Backlog. Demonstrates completed work to stakeholders and gathers feedback.
    👥 Stakeholder
    A person external to the Scrum Team with a specific interest in and knowledge of a product that is required for incremental discovery. Represented by the Product Owner and provides input during Sprint Review.
    📊 Story Points
    A unit of measure for expressing the overall effort required to fully implement a Product Backlog item or any other piece of work. Relative estimation rather than time-based.
    🔄 Scrum@Scale
    A scaling framework created by Jeff Sutherland that extends Scrum using a "scale-free" architecture. Uses Scrum of Scrums and Executive Action Teams to coordinate multiple teams while maintaining core Scrum principles.
    �️ SAFe (Scaled Agile Framework)
    A knowledge base of proven, integrated principles and practices for Lean-Agile development at enterprise scale. Created by Dean Leffingwell. Organises teams into Agile Release Trains (ARTs) operating on Program Increments (PIs). Available in four configurations: Essential SAFe, Large Solution SAFe, Portfolio SAFe, and Full SAFe. Built on the House of Lean and 10 Lean-Agile Principles. Current version: SAFe 6.0.
    🚞 Solution Train
    (SAFe) The organisational construct for building large and complex solutions that require multiple ARTs and Suppliers to coordinate. Used in Large Solution SAFe and Full SAFe configurations. Led by the Solution Train Engineer (STE). Coordinates through Solution-level PI Planning and Solution Demos. Examples: aircraft control systems, autonomous vehicle platforms, or enterprise-wide ERP programs.
    🎓 SPC (SAFe Program Consultant)
    (SAFe) A certified SAFe change agent trained to lead enterprise SAFe transformations. SPCs are qualified to coach, train, and guide organisations through the SAFe Implementation Roadmap. Must complete the Implementing SAFe (SPC) course. Required to teach most SAFe role-based certification courses. Typically among the first people trained in any SAFe adoption (Step 2 of the Implementation Roadmap).
    🏗️ System Architect / Engineer
    (SAFe) An ART-level role that defines, guides, and communicates the overall system architecture. Responsible for the Architectural Runway, cross-team technical alignment, quality standards, and NFR definition. Actively participates in PI Planning, System Demos, and Inspect & Adapt. Works closely with the RTE and Product Management to ensure technical decisions continuously enable business agility.
    💪 Self-Managing Team
    A team that internally decides who does what, when, and how. Replaces "Self-Organizing" in Scrum Guide 2020. Teams have autonomy and accountability to manage their own work within the boundaries of their authority.
    🔬 Spike
    A time-boxed research or experimentation task used to answer a question or gather information. Often used to reduce risk, investigate technical approaches, or better understand requirements before committing to implementation.
    🌱 Sustainable Pace
    A principle from Extreme Programming adopted by Agile teams. Teams should work at a pace they can sustain indefinitely without burnout. Prevents long-term productivity loss and maintains team health.
    🔀 Scrumban
    A hybrid approach combining Scrum's structured cadences, roles, Sprint commitments, and ceremonies with Kanban's explicit WIP limits, detailed workflow visualization, and flow metrics. Teams retain Sprint structure while adding WIP limits on Sprint board columns, cycle time tracking, and throughput-based forecasting. Coined by Corey Ladas (2008); formalized by Scrum.org's PSK curriculum.
    🏊 Swim Lane
    A horizontal row on a Kanban board that separates different categories of work, teams, or classes of service. Allows different types of work (e.g., Expedite, Standard, Tech Debt) to be managed simultaneously on the same board with different WIP limits and policies. Common swim lanes align with Kanban's four Classes of Service.

    T

    ⚙️ Technical Debt
    The implied cost of additional rework caused by choosing an easy solution now instead of using a better approach that would take longer. Should be tracked and addressed in the Product Backlog.
    🧪 Test-Driven Development (TDD)
    A software development practice where developers write automated tests before writing the production code. Follows the cycle: Red (write failing test), Green (write code to pass), Refactor (improve code quality).
    ⏰ Time-box
    A fixed period of time during which an activity is conducted. All Scrum events are time-boxed. Sprint is a time-box of one month or less.
    🔍 Transparency
    One of the three pillars of Scrum. Significant aspects of the process must be visible to those responsible for the outcome. Requires common standards so observers share a common understanding.
    🌲 Trunk-Based Development
    A source control practice where developers commit frequently to a single branch (trunk/main). Reduces merge conflicts, enables Continuous Integration, and supports faster delivery. Alternative to long-lived feature branches.
    📈 Throughput
    A primary Kanban flow metric measuring the number of work items completed per unit of time (e.g., 8 items/week). More stable and reliable than story-point velocity because it counts completed items regardless of estimated size. Foundation of Monte Carlo forecasting — as historical data grows, throughput-based forecasts become increasingly accurate. Per Little's Law: Cycle Time = WIP ÷ Throughput.

    U

    � Unrealized Value (UV)
    A Key Value Area in Evidence-Based Management that measures the potential value that could be realized if the product better met customer needs. Represents the gap between current state and ideal state.
    🎨 User Research
    The process of understanding user behaviors, needs, and motivations through observation techniques, task analysis, and other feedback methodologies. Essential for Product Discovery and informed decision-making.
    📝 User Story
    A short, simple description of a feature told from the perspective of the person who desires the new capability. Typically follows the format: "As a [type of user], I want [goal] so that [reason]."

    V

    💎 Value Stream
    The sequence of activities an organisation undertakes to deliver a product or service to a customer. Mapping value streams helps identify waste and optimise flow. In SAFe specifically: two types — Operational Value Streams (the steps to deliver value to end customers, e.g., order-to-cash) and Development Value Streams (the steps to build and maintain the systems that run operational value streams). Development value streams are funded through Lean Budgets and organised into ARTs.
    💪 Velocity
    The amount of Product Backlog work a Development Team can complete during a Sprint, measured in story points. Used for forecasting and capacity planning. Becomes more accurate over time.
    🔭 Vision (Product Vision)
    A clear, inspirational statement of what the product aims to achieve in the long term. Provides direction for the Product Goal and helps stakeholders understand the purpose. Created and communicated by the Product Owner.

    W

    🌊 Waterfall
    A traditional sequential software development approach where phases (requirements, design, implementation, testing, deployment) flow downward like a waterfall. Contrasts with Agile's iterative approach. Difficult to accommodate changes once a phase is complete.
    📊 WIP (Work in Progress)
    The amount of work that has been started but not yet completed. Limiting WIP is a key principle in Kanban and Lean, helping teams focus and improve flow. Reduces context switching and speeds up delivery.
    💼 Work-Life Balance
    The equilibrium between professional work and personal life. Modern Scrum teams prioritize work-life balance through sustainable pace, flexible working arrangements, and respect for personal time to prevent burnout and maintain long-term productivity.
    ⚖️ WSJF (Weighted Shortest Job First)
    (SAFe) SAFe's economic prioritisation model. Formula: WSJF = Cost of Delay ÷ Job Duration. Cost of Delay = User/Business Value + Time Criticality + Risk Reduction/Opportunity Enablement. Features and Epics with the highest WSJF score are sequenced first to maximise economic benefit. Replaces intuition-based priority ordering with an objective, quantified model. Job Duration is typically estimated in T-shirt sizes (S/M/L/XL) rather than story points.
