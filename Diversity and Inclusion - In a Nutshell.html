    ⚖️ Equity

    fairness justice

    Equity is the practice of ensuring fair treatment, equal access to opportunities, and impartial advancement for all people, while recognizing and addressing structural barriers, different needs, and historical disadvantages. Unlike equality (treating everyone the same), equity means treating everyone fairly based on their individual circumstances.

    Equity vs. Equality

    Equality Equity
    Treating everyone the same Treating everyone fairly based on needs
    Providing same resources to all Providing appropriate resources based on circumstances
    One-size-fits-all approach Individualized, context-aware approach
    Assumes level playing field Acknowledges systemic barriers and advantages
    Equal starting point Equal opportunity to succeed

    Core Principles of Equity

    • ✅ Fair Treatment: Ensuring everyone is treated justly, regardless of their background or identity
    • 🚧 Barrier Removal: Actively identifying and removing systemic, structural, and procedural obstacles that prevent full participation
    • 🔍 Needs Assessment: Recognizing that different people have different needs, circumstances, and starting points
    • 🎁 Individualized Support: Providing resources, accommodations, and opportunities based on individual and group circumstances
    • 📊 Data-Driven: Using metrics and evidence to identify disparities and measure progress
    • 🔄 Systemic Change: Addressing root causes rather than just symptoms of inequity

    Equity in Practice

    Workplace Examples

    • 💼 Hiring & Promotion: Structured interviews, diverse hiring panels, blind resume reviews to reduce bias
    • 💰 Compensation: Pay equity analyses, transparent salary bands, addressing historical wage gaps
    • 📚 Development: Mentorship programs for underrepresented groups, sponsorship initiatives, targeted leadership development
    • ⏰ Flexibility: Accommodating different work styles, caregiving responsibilities, religious observances, accessibility needs
    • 🏥 Benefits: Comprehensive healthcare including mental health, family planning, adoption support, transgender care
    • 🎯 Resource Allocation: Ensuring budget, tools, and opportunities are distributed based on need and impact

    Common Equity Challenges

    • 🔴 Unconscious Bias: Hidden preferences that affect decisions about hiring, promotion, and assignments
    • 🔴 Historical Disadvantages: Legacy systems and practices that perpetuate inequity
    • 🔴 Access Disparities: Unequal access to networks, information, mentorship, and opportunities
    • 🔴 Microaggressions: Small, often unintentional acts that communicate bias or exclusion
    • 🔴 Systemic Barriers: Policies and procedures that disproportionately impact certain groups

    Achieving Equity

    1. Acknowledge: Recognize that inequities exist and understand their historical context
    2. Assess: Collect and analyze data to identify disparities and their root causes
    3. Act: Implement targeted interventions and systemic changes
    4. Measure: Track progress using meaningful metrics and adjust strategies
    5. Sustain: Build equity into organizational culture, policies, and practices

    Fairness in opportunities and access. Equity requires ongoing commitment, resource investment, and willingness to change systems that perpetuate disparities. It's not about giving anyone an unfair advantage—it's about leveling the playing field so everyone can succeed based on their merit and effort.

    🫱🏽‍🫲🏻 Inclusion

    welcoming participation

    Inclusion is the practice of creating environments where all individuals feel welcomed, respected, supported, and valued as their authentic selves. It goes beyond simply having diversity—it means actively involving, engaging, and empowering all people to participate fully in organizational life.

    What Inclusion Looks Like

    Behavioral Indicators

    • 🏡 Welcoming Environment: Everyone feels comfortable being themselves without fear of judgment or exclusion
    • 📢 Voice & Influence: All perspectives are actively sought, heard, and integrated into decisions
    • 💚 Psychological Safety: People can take interpersonal risks, admit mistakes, and challenge ideas without fear
    • 🤲 Active Participation: Everyone is involved in meetings, projects, and decision-making processes
    • 🎯 Fair Treatment: Policies and practices are applied consistently and fairly across all groups
    • 🌟 Recognition: Contributions from all team members are acknowledged and celebrated

    Organizational Practices

    • 🗣️ Speaking Up Culture: Dissenting opinions and constructive challenges are welcomed
    • 👥 Diverse Representation: Leadership, teams, and decision-making bodies reflect diversity
    • 📚 Continuous Learning: Ongoing education about bias, privilege, and inclusive behaviors
    • 🛡️ Safe Reporting: Clear channels for reporting exclusion, bias, or discrimination without retaliation
    • 🎊 Celebration of Differences: Cultural events, awareness campaigns, and recognition of diverse holidays

    The Inclusion Experience

    Maureen Berkner Boyt's framework describes inclusion through four dimensions:

    Dimension Description Example
    Seen Being noticed and recognized Ideas are acknowledged in meetings; work is visible to leadership
    Heard Opinions are solicited and considered Input is actively requested; feedback influences decisions
    Valued Contributions are appreciated Unique perspectives are celebrated; expertise is utilized
    Supported Resources and advocacy are provided Development opportunities; sponsorship for advancement

    Barriers to Inclusion

    • ❌ Exclusive Language: Jargon, acronyms, or cultural references that exclude some people
    • ❌ Dominant Voices: Same people always speaking, interrupting, or getting credit
    • ❌ Homogeneous Networks: Informal networks and "in-groups" that exclude others
    • ❌ Microaggressions: Subtle put-downs or dismissals based on identity
    • ❌ Groupthink: Pressure to conform that silences diverse perspectives
    • ❌ Inaccessibility: Physical, technological, or procedural barriers to participation
    • ❌ Bias in Processes: Unexamined assumptions in hiring, promotion, and evaluation

    Building Inclusive Environments

    1. Lead with Intention: Leaders model inclusive behaviors and hold others accountable
    2. Design for Inclusion: Build inclusion into systems, not as an afterthought
    3. Create Psychological Safety: Make it safe to take risks, fail, and speak up
    4. Facilitate Participation: Use inclusive meeting practices and decision-making processes
    5. Address Exclusion: Intervene when exclusionary behaviors occur
    6. Measure and Improve: Track inclusion metrics and act on feedback

    Inclusive Meeting Practices

    • 📋 Share agendas in advance with opportunity for input
    • 🎤 Use round-robin or structured turn-taking to ensure all voices heard
    • ✋ Establish ground rules against interrupting and dominating
    • 💭 Provide multiple ways to contribute (verbal, chat, post-meeting)
    • ⏰ Start and end on time to respect everyone's schedules
    • 📊 Share materials in accessible formats
    • 🌍 Be mindful of time zones and cultural considerations

    Welcoming and valuing everyone. Inclusion is an ongoing practice, not a destination. It requires daily mindfulness, active effort, and commitment from everyone—especially those with power and privilege. When done well, inclusion transforms diverse groups into high-performing teams where innovation thrives.

    🏠 Belonging

    connection authenticity

    Belonging is the emotional experience of being valued, accepted, and included as your full, authentic self. It's the feeling that "I can be me here" and "I matter to this group." Belonging is the ultimate outcome when identity is honored, diversity is present, equity is practiced, and inclusion is lived.

    What Belonging Feels Like

    When people experience belonging at work, they report feeling:

    • 😊 Authenticity: Comfortable being yourself without code-switching, masking, or hiding parts of your identity
    • 🫂 Connection: Genuine relationships with colleagues; feeling part of a community, not just a workplace
    • 🛡️ Psychological Safety: Able to share ideas, admit mistakes, ask questions, and take interpersonal risks without fear
    • ⭐ Value: Your unique contributions, perspectives, and presence are actively appreciated
    • 🎖️ Recognition: Achievements are acknowledged; you're seen as integral to team success
    • 💪 Empowerment: Agency to influence decisions, shape your work, and impact outcomes
    • 🌟 Growth: Supported in your development; failures are learning opportunities
    • 🎯 Purpose: Your work has meaning; you see how you contribute to something larger

    Why Belonging Matters

    Individual Impact

    • 🧠 Mental Health: Reduced stress, anxiety, and burnout; improved well-being
    • 💼 Performance: Higher engagement, productivity, and quality of work
    • 🚀 Innovation: More willing to share ideas, take creative risks, and innovate
    • 🎓 Learning: Greater openness to feedback and continuous development
    • 😊 Job Satisfaction: Higher happiness and fulfillment at work

    Organizational Impact

    • 📊 Business Results: 56% increase in job performance, 50% lower turnover risk (BetterUp research)
    • 🤝 Collaboration: Stronger teamwork, knowledge sharing, and cross-functional partnerships
    • 💡 Innovation Rate: Teams with high belonging are 3x more likely to innovate
    • 🎯 Retention: Employees with strong belonging are 3x more likely to stay
    • ⚡ Agility: Faster adaptation to change; greater organizational resilience

    • ⚠️ Separate social groups that don't intermingle
    • ⚠️ Code-switching or "covering" behaviors
    • ⚠️ Lack of constructive challenge or dissenting opinions
    • ⚠️ People "quiet quitting" or doing minimum required work
    • ⚠️ Exclusion from informal networks and social events
    • ⚠️ Difficulty providing honest feedback

    As a Leader

    • 🎯 Model vulnerability and authenticity
    • 🎯 Create opportunities for connection beyond work tasks
    • 🎯 Actively combat "othering" and exclusionary behaviors
    • 🎯 Celebrate both individual uniqueness and collective identity

    • 💙 Learn about colleagues' backgrounds, interests, and perspectives
    • 💙 Speak up when you see exclusion or bias
    • 💙 Share your authentic self and create space for others to do the same
    • 💙 Build bridges across difference; seek out diverse perspectives

    As an Organization

    • 🏢 Address systemic barriers to belonging
    • 🏢 Create Employee Resource Groups (ERGs) and affinity spaces

    🔍 #1: Examine Your Assumptions

    self-awareness critical thinking

    Challenge your own thinking and actively question the basis of your beliefs about others. Our brains naturally make quick assumptions to process information efficiently, but these shortcuts can lead to unfair judgments and exclusionary behaviors.

    flowchart TD A[💭 Notice Your Thought] --> B{Is this an assumption?} B -->|Yes| C[🔍 Question It] B -->|No| D[✅ Proceed] C --> E{Do I have evidence?} E -->|No| F[📚 Learn More] E -->|Partial| G[🤔 Seek to Understand] E -->|Yes| H[✅ Validated] F --> I[💬 Ask Questions] G --> I I --> J[🎯 Adjust Your Perspective] style A fill:#dbeafe,stroke:#3b82f6,stroke-width:2px style C fill:#fef3c7,stroke:#f59e0b,stroke-width:2px style J fill:#dcfce7,stroke:#10b981,stroke-width:2px

    Why This Matters

    Unchecked assumptions can lead to:

    • Bias in decisions: Hiring, promotions, and project assignments based on false beliefs
    • Exclusion: Leaving people out because of what you assume about them
    • Missed opportunities: Overlooking talent and ideas
    • Damaged relationships: Acting on incorrect beliefs erodes trust

    How to Practice

    • ❓ Identify your assumptions: Notice when you're making judgments about someone's capabilities, interests, or background
      • Example: "They probably wouldn't want to lead this project" → Why do I think that?
    • ✓ Test your assumptions: Do you have actual evidence, or are you relying on stereotypes?
      • Example: Instead of assuming, ask: "Would you be interested in leading this project?"
    • 🔎 Seek information: Replace assumptions with questions and curiosity
      • Example: "I'd like to understand your perspective on this. Can you share your thoughts?"

    Real-World Examples

    Assumption Better Approach
    "They're too junior to contribute to strategy" "Let me invite them and see what fresh perspectives they bring"
    "Working parents can't handle travel-heavy roles" "I'll discuss their preferences and constraints directly"
    "They won't fit in with the team culture" "Different perspectives strengthen team culture"

    Common Mistakes to Avoid

    • ❌ Using "I'm just being realistic" to justify biased assumptions
    • ❌ Assuming everyone wants the same things you do
    • ❌ Making generalizations based on one or two experiences
    • ❌ Letting first impressions solidify into unchangeable beliefs

    Pro Tip: Start each day by identifying one assumption you're making, then actively challenge it by gathering real information.

    • 🤔 What would I advise a friend in this situation?
    • 🤔 What bias might be influencing my perspective?

    💬 Microaggressions

    awareness impact

    Microaggressions are brief, everyday exchanges that send denigrating messages to certain individuals because of their group membership. Often unintentional, these comments and actions communicate hostile, derogatory, or negative slights and insults. The term was coined by psychiatrist Chester Pierce in 1970.

    Types of Microaggressions

    Type Description Example
    Microassaults Explicit, deliberate discriminatory actions Using racial slurs, displaying discriminatory symbols
    Microinsults Subtle snubs that convey rudeness or insensitivity "You're so articulate" (implying surprise), "Where are you really from?"
    Microinvalidations Comments that negate or dismiss experiences "I don't see color," "That's not racist," "You're being too sensitive"

    Common Examples by Category

    Race and Ethnicity

    • ❌ "Where are you really from?" (implies not belonging)
    • ❌ "You speak English so well!" (assumes incompetence)
    • ❌ Touching someone's hair without permission
    • ❌ "I don't see color" (denies reality of racialized experiences)
    • ❌ Clutching purse when person of color approaches
    • ❌ Mistaking someone for the only other person of their race

    Gender

    • ❌ "You're pretty smart for a girl"
    • ❌ Interrupting or talking over women in meetings
    • ❌ Asking women to take notes or plan parties
    • ❌ "Are you sure you can handle this?" (questioning competence)
    • ❌ Comments about appearance instead of work contributions
    • ❌ "She's emotional" vs. "He's passionate"

    LGBTQ+

    • ❌ "That's so gay" (using identity as insult)
    • ❌ "What's your real name?" (to transgender individuals)
    • ❌ "You don't look gay"
    • ❌ "Who's the man/woman in the relationship?"
    • ❌ "It's just a phase"
    • ❌ Assuming everyone has opposite-sex partners

    Disability

    • ❌ "You're so inspiring!" (for doing ordinary things)
    • ❌ Speaking to an interpreter instead of the deaf person
    • ❌ "I forgot you were in a wheelchair"
    • ❌ Using parking spaces or accessible facilities without need
    • ❌ "You don't look disabled"
    • ❌ Assuming people with disabilities can't contribute fully

    Why Microaggressions Matter

    Cumulative Impact

    • ☔ Death by a Thousand Cuts: Individual incidents seem small, but accumulate over time
    • ⚡ Constant Vigilance: Targets must always be on guard, leading to exhaustion
    • 🏥 Health Effects: Chronic stress, anxiety, depression, and physical health impacts
    • 💼 Career Impact: Reduced job satisfaction, performance, and advancement
    • 🧠 Cognitive Load: Mental energy spent processing and responding reduces available capacity

    Responding to Microaggressions

    If You Experience One

    • Assess Safety: Determine if it's safe to address in the moment
    • Decide Response: Address immediately, later, or not at all—all are valid choices
    • Possible Responses:
      • Ask for clarification: "What did you mean by that?"
      • State impact: "That comment made me uncomfortable because..."
      • Educate: "You might not realize, but that phrase..."
      • Redirect: "Let's focus on my qualifications"
    • Seek Support: Talk to trusted colleagues, HR, or employee assistance programs
    • Document: Keep records of patterns for potential formal complaints

    If You Witness One

    • Interrupt: "Hold on, let's reconsider that comment"
    • Question: "I'm confused—why would you say that?"
    • Educate: "That language can be hurtful because..."
    • Support Target: Check in privately: "Are you okay? That wasn't okay"
    • Report if Needed: Escalate serious or pattern issues

    If You Commit One

    • 1. Acknowledge: "Thank you for pointing that out"
    • 2. Apologize: "I'm sorry for that comment/action"
    • 3. Don't Center Yourself: Avoid "I didn't mean it" or "I'm not racist/sexist"
    • 4. Learn: Educate yourself about why it was problematic
    • 5. Do Better: Change behavior going forward
    • 6. Don't Expect Forgiveness: The harmed person doesn't owe you absolution

    Prevention Strategies

    • ✅ Educate yourself about different cultures, identities, and experiences
    • ✅ Examine your own biases and assumptions
    • ✅ Listen more than you speak, especially to marginalized voices
    • ✅ Think before speaking—consider potential impact
    • ✅ Avoid assumptions about people based on appearance
    • ✅ Use inclusive language consistently
    • ✅ Create team norms that discourage microaggressions

    Intent vs. Impact: Good intentions don't negate harmful impact. When someone tells you a comment hurt them, believe and respect their experience. The goal is to cause less harm, not to defend yourself.

    🤲 Allyship

    advocacy solidarity

    Allyship is the ongoing practice of using your privilege, power, and influence to advocate for and support marginalized individuals and communities. Effective allyship requires action, not just stated support—it's a verb, not a noun. You don't "become" an ally; you practice allyship through consistent behavior.

    Core Principles of Allyship

    Characteristics of Effective Allyship

    Do This ✅ Not This ❌
    Listen to marginalized people's experiences Speak over or for marginalized people
    Amplify marginalized voices Take credit for others' ideas or work
    Educate yourself independently Expect marginalized people to educate you
    Accept and learn from criticism Get defensive or make it about your feelings
    Use your privilege to challenge bias Stay silent to avoid discomfort
    Take action even when it's uncomfortable Only support when it's convenient or easy
    Follow the leadership of those affected Decide what marginalized people need without asking
    Acknowledge mistakes and do better Make excuses or demand forgiveness

    The Spectrum of Allyship Actions

    Level 1: Awareness & Learning

    • 📚 Educate yourself about different identities, histories, and experiences
    • 🎧 Listen to marginalized voices through books, podcasts, articles
    • 🔍 Examine your own privilege and biases
    • 💭 Reflect on how systems benefit you and harm others

    Level 2: Personal Practice

    • 🗣️ Use inclusive language consistently
    • 👥 Diversify your social circles and media consumption
    • 🤐 Check your tendency to dominate conversations
    • 💬 Practice having uncomfortable conversations about bias

    Level 3: Speaking Up

    • 🚫 Interrupt discriminatory jokes, comments, and behaviors
    • 💪 Correct people who misgender or use wrong names
    • 📢 Challenge bias in meetings and group settings
    • ✋ Don't laugh at or ignore problematic comments

    Level 4: Active Advocacy

    • 🎤 Amplify marginalized voices in meetings and discussions
    • 🙋 Recommend qualified candidates from underrepresented groups
    • 🤝 Mentor and sponsor people from marginalized communities
    • 👏 Give credit to those who deserve it

    Level 5: Systemic Change

    • 🔨 Advocate for policy changes that promote equity
    • 📊 Question and address biased systems and processes
    • 💰 Allocate resources to DEI initiatives
    • ⚖️ Hold people and organizations accountable
    • 🏢 Use your influence to create institutional change

    Allyship in Practice

    In Meetings

    • ✅ Notice who's speaking and who's being interrupted—intervene
    • ✅ "Let's hear from [name] who hasn't had a chance to speak"
    • ✅ "I want to make sure we heard what [name] just said..."
    • ✅ Give credit: "As [name] suggested earlier..."
    • ✅ Challenge ideas, not people, and create space for dissent

    In Hiring and Promotion

    • ✅ Advocate for diverse candidate pools
    • ✅ Challenge biased assessment criteria
    • ✅ Sponsor high-potential employees from underrepresented groups
    • ✅ Question why leadership lacks diversity
    • ✅ Support equitable compensation and advancement

    In Daily Interactions

    • ✅ Share your pronouns to normalize the practice
    • ✅ Ask people their preferences rather than assuming
    • ✅ Learn correct pronunciation of names
    • ✅ Don't expect marginalized people to represent entire groups
    • ✅ Check in privately with people after difficult situations

    Common Allyship Mistakes

    Mistake Why It's Problematic Better Approach
    Performative Allyship Shows support publicly but doesn't take real action Take meaningful action even when no one's watching
    Centering Yourself Makes allyship about you and your feelings Center the experiences and needs of marginalized people
    Savior Complex Assumes marginalized people need to be "saved" Support their leadership and agency
    Expecting Praise Seeks recognition for basic decency Do the work because it's right, not for accolades
    Giving Up After Criticism Centers your discomfort over continued harm Accept feedback, learn, and keep trying

    Self-Assessment Questions

    • ❓ Am I listening more than I'm talking?
    • ❓ Whose voices am I amplifying?
    • ❓ Am I taking action when it's uncomfortable or inconvenient?
    • ❓ Do I follow the leadership of those most affected?
    • ❓ Am I educating myself or expecting others to teach me?
    • ❓ When I make mistakes, do I accept accountability?
    • ❓ Am I using my privilege to challenge systems, not just individuals?
    • ❓ Is my support consistent or only when it's visible?

    Allyship is a Practice, Not an Identity: You can't declare yourself an ally—marginalized communities decide who their allies are based on your actions. Focus on being a good accomplice: showing up, taking risks, and transferring power and resources to those who need them.

    🧠 Unconscious Bias

    awareness mitigation

    Unconscious bias (also called implicit bias) refers to the attitudes, stereotypes, and associations we hold about people and groups that operate outside our conscious awareness. These biases are formed through exposure to cultural messages, personal experiences, and societal conditioning, and they influence our decisions and behaviors automatically.

    How Unconscious Bias Works

    Common Types of Unconscious Bias

    Bias Type Description Example
    Affinity Bias Preference for people similar to ourselves Hiring candidates who went to your college or share your hobbies
    Confirmation Bias Seeking information that confirms existing beliefs Noticing when a team member you like succeeds, ignoring their mistakes
    Attribution Bias Attributing success/failure differently based on group His success is skill; her success is luck
    Halo Effect One positive trait influences overall impression Assuming attractive people are also competent
    Horns Effect One negative trait influences overall impression Dismissing all ideas from someone who made one mistake
    Gender Bias Stereotypes about gender capabilities and roles Assuming women are better at administrative tasks, men at technical
    Name Bias Assumptions based on names Resumes with "white-sounding" names get more callbacks
    Ageism Stereotypes about capabilities based on age Assuming older workers can't learn technology, younger workers lack experience
    Beauty Bias Preference for conventionally attractive people More attractive candidates rated as more competent
    Conformity Bias Pressure to match group opinions Changing your assessment to match the team's consensus

    Impact of Unconscious Bias

    In Hiring

    • ⚠️ Identical resumes get different responses based on names suggesting race/gender
    • ⚠️ Interview questions and assessments vary by candidate demographics
    • ⚠️ "Culture fit" often means "similar to existing team"
    • ⚠️ Leadership potential assessed differently across gender/race

    In Performance Evaluation

    • ⚠️ Women receive more personality-focused feedback; men receive more task-focused feedback
    • ⚠️ People of color held to higher performance standards
    • ⚠️ Same behavior described differently: "assertive" vs. "aggressive," "confident" vs. "arrogant"
    • ⚠️ Recency bias affects ratings (recent events weighted more heavily)

    In Career Development

    • ⚠️ Sponsorship and high-visibility assignments go to those similar to leaders
    • ⚠️ Women and people of color receive less developmental feedback
    • ⚠️ Assumptions about commitment affect opportunities offered
    • ⚠️ "Proven track record" requirements disadvantage those historically excluded

    Mitigating Unconscious Bias

    Individual Strategies

    • ✅ Awareness: Take implicit association tests; learn about your biases
    • ✅ Slow Down: Bias thrives in quick decisions; take time when possible
    • ✅ Question Assumptions: Ask yourself "What am I assuming and why?"
    • ✅ Consider Alternatives: "What other explanations could there be?"
    • ✅ Increase Contact: Build relationships across difference
    • ✅ Counter-Stereotypic Imaging: Consciously think of counter-examples to stereotypes
    • ✅ Perspective-Taking: Imagine experiences from others' viewpoints

    Organizational Strategies

    • ✅ Structured Processes: Use standardized interviews, rubrics, and evaluation criteria
    • ✅ Blind Review: Remove identifying information from resumes/applications when possible
    • ✅ Diverse Panels: Include multiple perspectives in hiring and promotion decisions
    • ✅ Data Analysis: Regularly analyze outcomes across demographics to spot patterns
    • ✅ Clear Criteria: Define requirements and qualifications objectively before evaluating candidates
    • ✅ Accountability: Track and report on diversity outcomes; hold leaders accountable
    • ✅ Training: Provide evidence-based bias training (though training alone isn't sufficient)

    Important Realities About Bias

    What Doesn't Work

    • ❌ Simply being aware of bias doesn't eliminate it
    • ❌ One-time training has limited lasting impact
    • ❌ Trying to "not be biased" can backfire
    • ❌ Believing you're objective makes you more biased ("bias blind spot")

    What Does Work

    • ✅ Structural changes that limit opportunities for bias to influence decisions
    • ✅ Ongoing practice and habit formation
    • ✅ Accountability through measurement and transparency
    • ✅ Creating environments where people can call out bias without retaliation
    • ✅ Combination of individual awareness and systemic change

    Self-Reflection Questions

    • 🤔 Who do I naturally gravitate toward? Who do I overlook?
    • 🤔 Whose ideas do I immediately trust vs. question?
    • 🤔 Who gets the benefit of the doubt? Who doesn't?
    • 🤔 What assumptions am I making about this person's capabilities?
    • 🤔 Would I evaluate this the same way if the person were a different gender/race?
    • 🤔 Am I holding different people to different standards?
    • 🤔 What counter-evidence am I ignoring to maintain my current view?

    Everyone Has Bias: Having unconscious bias doesn't make you a bad person—it makes you human. What matters is acknowledging your biases, understanding their impact, and actively working to prevent them from causing harm. The goal isn't perfection; it's continuous improvement.
